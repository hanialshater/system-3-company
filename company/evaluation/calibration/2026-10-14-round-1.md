# Calibration: pairwise round 1 (2026-10-14)

owner: head-of-evaluation (Tobias) · records: [`../pairwise/2026-10-14-round-1.jsonl`](../pairwise/2026-10-14-round-1.jsonl) · round record: [`../rounds/2026-10-14-round-1.md`](../rounds/2026-10-14-round-1.md) · plan: [`../rounds/round-1/plan.md`](../rounds/round-1/plan.md)

**Read this first.** There are 5 repeats × 6 criteria = 30 repeated comparisons, from 8 judges who are all models from the same family as the builders. One flip moves a rate by about 3 points. Every number below is a rough signal, not a measured judge property.

## Method

Every comparison is mapped to **slugs**, not to A/B positions, before it is compared. A swapped repeat "agrees" when the same *lesson* wins (so `b` in the original and `a` in the swap is agreement). Outcomes:
- **agree**: same lesson won, or both said tie;
- **half**: one said tie and the other picked a lesson;
- **flip**: opposite lessons won.

## Agreement

| set | agree | half | flip | strict agreement |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| all 5 repeats (30) | 24 | 5 | 1 | **24/30 = 80%** (tool: `agreement.rate` 0.8, same number) |
| same-order second judge (12) | 9 | 3 | 0 | 9/12 = 75% |
| A/B swapped, different judge (18) | 15 | 2 | 1 | 15/18 = 83% |

- **Only one outright flip in 30.** Every disagreement except one is "one judge saw a difference, the other called it a tie". Judges disagree about *whether* two lessons separate far more than about *which* one is better.
- By criterion: prediction 5/5 and clarity 4/5 agree (clarity holds the one flip). Learning 3/5, engagement 4/5, shown-not-told 4/5 and trust 4/5 lose one or two to ties.
- The one flip (pair-01 vs pair-20, clarity) is a real difference of reading, not noise. judge-4 found penalty-kick-mix's payoff table "rendered in tiny type", and judge-6 found mild-preferences-sharp-divides' card "dense" with six buttons and a status line. Both observations are true of the artifacts. Clarity on a phone is the criterion where two careful readers weigh different defects.
- pair-24 vs pair-16 is the weakest repeat: judge-2 tied three criteria that judge-6 decided. judge-2 also has the highest tie rate in the round (6/18 lines). Read this as a judge threshold for "separates", not as contradiction.
- Against the pattern's test (*are pairwise verdicts consistent across independent judges?*): 80% strict, 97% without an outright flip. That is encouraging but comes from 5 pairs, so it is not yet evidence for `company/knowledge/patterns/pairwise-not-scores.md`. There is no absolute-score baseline to compare against.

## Position bias

**Swap test (the direct measure).** 3 swaps × 6 criteria = 18 comparisons. 15 agree, 2 half and 1 flips. The one flip went to the lesson shown as **B** in the repeat, not A. So the swaps show **no measurable position bias** (flip rate 1/18 ≈ 6%, which is within the 0/12 flips plus 3/12 halves of the same-order baseline). Because a different judge did each swap, a flip would mix position bias with judge disagreement. With none in the A direction, that does not matter here.

**Raw A vs B (the indirect signal).** Over all 144 lines:

| | A won | B won | tie | A share of decisive |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| all 144 | 75 | 52 | 17 | 59% (two-sided binomial p ≈ 0.05, lines treated as independent, which they are not) |
| 19 base pairs only (114) | 57 | 47 | 10 | 55% (p ≈ 0.38) |
| by pair (majority of 6 lines) | 15 pairs | 9 pairs | 0 | 62% (p ≈ 0.31) |
| prediction only | **18** | **4** | 2 | **82%** (p ≈ 0.004 unadjusted; ≈ 0.03 after six-criterion Bonferroni) |
| learning | 12 | 8 | 4 | 60% |
| shown-not-told | 11 | 9 | 4 | 55% |
| clarity | 11 | 13 | 0 | 46% |
| engagement | 13 | 10 | 1 | 57% |
| trust | 10 | 8 | 6 | 56% |

By judge, decisive A:B was judge-7 15:1, judge-5 14:4, judge-6 12:6, judge-3 9:7, judge-8 7:6, judge-1 8:9, judge-2 4:8, judge-4 6:11.

**Reading.** Overall, A wins somewhat more often, but per pair (the real unit) it is 15 vs 9, which is not distinguishable from chance. The **prediction** criterion is the one real flag: A won 18 of 22 decisive verdicts. Two things argue against a pure position effect there. First, all three swaps agreed on prediction, each time following the lesson and not the slot. Second, the losing reasons are concrete and checkable: "the locked stage already shows the arrows a careful learner could count", "makes the intuitive first option correct". The A/B order came from a seeded LCG, and the base cycle happens to put several lessons whose questions judges called give-aways (news-comes-from-acquaintances, prices-sort-the-buyers, web-bow-tie-reach) in slot B. judge-7's 15:1 is mostly pair-09 (prices-sort-the-buyers lost all 6 lines, and judge-6 independently agreed 6/6 in pair-23). **Verdict: no position bias shown by the swap test; prediction's A-skew is unexplained and is watched next round.** Round 2 should give prediction more swaps (see the round record).

## Tie rate

17/144 = **11.8%** of lines. By criterion: trust 6, learning 4, shown-not-told 4, prediction 2, engagement 1, clarity 0. By judge: judge-2 6/18, judge-8 5/18, judge-3 2, judge-7 2, judges 1/4 one each, judges 5/6 none. Trust ties are mostly "both reveals end with a visible toy-model line", which suggests most NCM lessons now carry a limits line and trust separates them only when one lacks it. Clarity never tied. That suggests judges always find a phone-readability difference, which may be real (the cards differ a lot in density) or may be forced. Watch it.

## Length bias

Lengths are the byte sizes of the **packet** transcripts the judges saw (`artifacts/pairwise/round-1/pair-NN/{A,B}/transcript.md`, trust-sheet appendix removed). Raw capture sizes include the redacted appendix and would mislead.

| | won by the longer transcript | won by the shorter | share to longer |
|---|---|---|---|
| all decisive lines (127) | 39 | 88 | **31%** |
| clarity | 2 | 22 | 8% |
| shown-not-told | 5 | 15 | 25% |
| engagement | 8 | 15 | 35% |
| prediction | 8 | 14 | 36% |
| learning | 8 | 12 | 40% |
| trust | 8 | 10 | 44% |

The usual worry with model judges is that longer wins. Here **shorter wins** two times in three, and on clarity almost always. That is plausibly a true signal: a longer transcript means more on-card text and more steps, which is what "easier to follow on a phone" penalises, and the clarity reasons name density directly. But length and the judgement cannot be separated with this design. Length is not controlled, and it is not a simple rule: the two longest packet transcripts (cluster-stops-the-cascade 6.9 KB, enemy-of-my-enemy 6.1 KB) sit low on most criteria, but below-critical-mass (4.9 KB) and mild-preferences-sharp-divides (5.3 KB) sit near the top, and the shortest (agenda-picks-the-winner, 2.3 KB) sits near the top too. Report: **the judges show a preference for shorter cards, strongest on clarity. Not a verbosity bias. Possibly a brevity bias.**

## The blinding issue (claim counter and badge)

Every judge reported that each screenshot shows an "IN REVIEW" status badge and a "Why trust this? (k/n claims checked)" counter that differs between lessons. Only two lessons showed an incomplete count at capture: two-said-blue@v5 (8/9) and news-comes-from-acquaintances@v3 (4/5). All others showed n/n. judge-1 cited the lower count in two trust reasons (round-1-42, pair-07; round-1-108, pair-18). The lesson with the full count won the trust line in all three pairs that set a full-count lesson against one of these two (05, 07, 18).

This is not a protocol rule 3 leak (no authorship, recency or rank), so the lines stay `"blind": true`. But a checked-claims count is fact-check *process state*, not something the card states. It can correlate with how recently a lesson was revised. Decision and fix are in the round record. In short: **round 1 trust is flagged "possibly anchored on claim counts"**, no line is excluded, and future packets blank the badge and the counter. Sensitivity: refitting trust without round-1-42 and round-1-108 moves two-said-blue from 13th to 10th and news-comes-from-acquaintances from 17th to 15th. Both moves are inside their intervals, and the top 5 is unchanged except for order.

## What this does not show

- Whether any lesson teaches better. These are model preferences (decision record, objection 2).
- Judge-level reliability: each judge did 3 pairs, and each judge pair shares one repeat.
- Cross-family bias: no judge from another model family was used.
