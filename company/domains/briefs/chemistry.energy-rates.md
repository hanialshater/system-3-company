Every SVG label and readout at least 10 px (the rendered size at 390 px width, not the viewBox size); tap targets 44 px (`.tap`).
- **QA path.** Every milestone is reached by pressing each stage control once in DOM order (preset buttons or one-press steps, never a slider that needs many presses), and the question's own case is a milestone. Each brief traces that path. Every control changes the stage when pressed.
- **Claims.** Support is derivation (arithmetic the fact-checker redoes in node), simulation (a script the verifier re-runs) or citation. CRC = *CRC Handbook of Chemistry and Physics*; Atkins = Atkins & de Paula, *Physical Chemistry*; CODATA = CODATA Key Values for Thermodynamics (Cox, Wagman & Medvedev, 1989). Derivations are preferred because many web hosts are blocked here; every citation names a book table a checker can use offline, or is backed by a derivation. Every number below was run in node on 2026-10-14; the builder re-runs the Check.
- **Safety.** No brief asks or hints that the learner try anything at home. Hazardous materials (hydrogen, sodium hydroxide, kilns, boiling water) appear only on the stage.
- **Evidence.** No simulated review counts as learning evidence for any of these.

### 8. hydrogen-per-litre · concept: energy-density · core · demand 3 · format: Single card
- Learner knows: that burning a fuel releases energy (pay-first-then-profit); that a gas takes far more room than a liquid (one-ml-of-steam) / does not know: that "energy per kilogram" and "energy per litre" can rank fuels in opposite orders, and that hydrogen wins one and loses the other by a factor of thousands.
- Misconception: hydrogen is simply the best fuel, so a tank of it holds more energy than the same tank of petrol.
- Prediction (stage text <= 25 words): "Per kilogram, burning hydrogen gives about 3 times the energy of petrol. Per litre, as gas at room pressure, hydrogen gives..." options: A. about 3 times more / B. about the same / C. about 30 times less / D. about 3,000 times less (answer: D, 2,880 times less).
- Check (node): octane stands in for petrol. ΔcH(octane, l) = 8(−393.51) + 9(−285.83) − (−250.1) = −5,470 kJ/mol; / 114.23 g/mol = 47.9 MJ/kg. Hydrogen: 285.83 kJ/mol / 2.016 g/mol = 141.8 MJ/kg; ratio 2.96. Hydrogen gas at 25 °C, 1 atm: ρ = PM/RT = 101,325 x 0.002016 / (8.314 x 298.15) = 0.0824 g/L, so 11.7 kJ/L. Octane, ρ about 0.703 kg/L: 33.7 MJ/L. Ratio per litre 2,881. Liquid hydrogen (about 70.8 g/L at −253 °C): 10.0 MJ/L, still 3.4 times less than octane.
- Locked stage: one horizontal log axis of energy (1 kJ to 100 MJ, a gridline per power of ten, labels at 10^3, 10^5, 10^7 J), two empty bar slots labelled "petrol (octane)" and "hydrogen", a toggle row "per kg / per litre" with nothing selected. No bar values on the locked stage.
- What the stage shows (explore): control 1 "Per kilogram" draws both bars (47.9 MJ against 142 MJ, hydrogen ahead, readout "hydrogen x3.0"); control 2 "Per litre (gas, 1 atm)" redraws both bars per litre (33.7 MJ against 11.7 kJ), the hydrogen bar falling more than three gridlines, readout "petrol x2,900"; control 3 "Liquefy hydrogen (−253 °C)" adds a ghost bar for liquid hydrogen at 10.0 MJ/L, readout "petrol x3.4". Milestones: (1) per kg seen, (2) per litre gas seen (the question's case, ratio recorded), (3) liquid hydrogen seen. QA path: controls in that DOM order reach all three; the per-litre bars stay drawn under the ghost bar.
- Key state: per-litre bars on screen with the ratio "x2,900" recorded at milestone 2.
- Reveal draft (76 words): "Per kilogram hydrogen won, about 142 MJ against 48 MJ for petrol. Per litre it lost by about {2,900} times: a litre of hydrogen gas at room pressure weighs only 0.08 g, so it carries about 12 kJ, while a litre of petrol carries about 34 MJ. Even liquefied at −253 °C, hydrogen holds about 3 times less per litre. Which fuel is "best" depends on whether you are short of mass or short of space."
- Phone fit: the reveal names both per-kg values, the per-litre ratio and both per-litre values, and the liquid ghost bar; the axis, the three bars and the ratio readout must be on screen with stage-top crop 0. One axis, one readout row, three controls.
- Planned claims:
  1. Burning hydrogen to liquid water releases about 286 kJ/mol, about 142 MJ per kilogram. support: derivation from a cited constant; source: ΔfH(H2O, l) = −285.83 kJ/mol (CODATA; CRC), molar mass 2.016 g/mol (IUPAC 2021), node arithmetic.
  2. Burning octane releases about 5,470 kJ/mol, about 48 MJ per kilogram. support: derivation; source: ΔfH(CO2, g) −393.51 and ΔfH(H2O, l) −285.83 kJ/mol (CODATA), ΔfH(n-octane, l) about −250 kJ/mol (CRC, "Standard thermodynamic properties of chemical substances"); cross-check the CRC heat of combustion of n-octane (about −5,470 kJ/mol). Both values are higher heating values (water formed as liquid), stated on the stage caption.
  3. Hydrogen gas at 25 °C and 1 atm has a density of about 0.082 g/L. support: derivation; source: ideal gas law, R = 8.314 J/(mol K); hydrogen is close to ideal at 1 atm.
  4. Octane's density is about 0.70 kg/L and liquid hydrogen's about 71 g/L (at its boiling point, about −253 °C). support: citation; source: CRC (physical constants of organic compounds; properties of cryogenic fluids). Assumption disclosed on the stage: octane stands in for petrol, a mixture with a similar energy per kilogram.
  5. Per litre, octane carries about 2,900 times the energy of hydrogen gas at 1 atm and about 3.4 times that of liquid hydrogen. support: derivation; source: claims 1-4, node arithmetic.
- Metadata for meta.json: `also`: ["chemistry:bond-energy-enthalpy", "chemistry:ideal-gas-law", "chemistry:density"]; references: none.
- curriculum: `{"id":"energy-density","name":"Energy per kilogram vs energy per litre","level":"core","demand":3,"prerequisites":["bond-energy-enthalpy","ideal-gas-law"]}`
- Needs interaction-designer spec: no.
- Differs from: `one-gram-two-fires` (nuclear-vs-chemical-energy) compares nuclear and chemical energy per gram; `pay-first-then-profit` balances bond energies in one reaction; `one-ml-of-steam` is the liquid-gas volume ratio of one substance. This is the only lesson where the ranking of two fuels flips with the unit. It makes no claim about vehicles, tanks at 700 bar or climate; a 700-bar case needs a non-ideal density with its own source.
- Safety: hydrogen and petrol appear only on the stage; no handling or burning is suggested.

### 11. heat-the-ammonia-maker · concept: temperature-shifts-equilibrium · core · demand 2 · format: Single card
- Learner knows: that heating speeds reactions up (twice-as-hot-not-twice-as-fast, the-tail-that-reacts); that an equilibrium settles where forward and backward rates match (four-to-one-settles); that squeezing shifts an equilibrium (squeeze-the-brown-gas) / does not know: that temperature changes the equilibrium constant itself, and for a reaction that releases heat, heating lowers how much product there is at the end.
- Misconception: heating helps every reaction, so a hotter ammonia reactor ends with more ammonia.
- Prediction (stage text <= 25 words): "N2 + 3H2 ⇌ 2NH3 releases heat. Heat it from 25 °C to 450 °C and let it settle. The ammonia at equilibrium..." options: A. rises a lot / B. stays about the same / C. falls a lot (answer: C; K falls from about 6 x 10^5 to about 2 x 10^-4).
- Check (node): ΔH° = 2(−45.94) = −91.88 kJ/mol; ΔS° = 2(192.77) − 191.61 − 3(130.68) = −198.1 J/(mol K). Assuming ΔH° and ΔS° stay constant (stated): K = exp(−(ΔH° − TΔS°)/RT). 25 °C: ΔG° = −32.8 kJ/mol, K = 5.6 x 10^5. 200 °C: ΔG° = +1.9, K = 0.62. 450 °C: ΔG° = +51.4, K = 1.9 x 10^-4. K = 1 near 191 °C. Factor 25 °C to 450 °C: about 3 x 10^9.
- Locked stage: a vertical log axis of K (10^-5 to 10^6, gridlines per power of ten, labelled every other one), a thermometer column with three unpressed preset chips "25 °C / 200 °C / 450 °C", caption "K large = mostly ammonia at the end". No point plotted.
- What the stage shows (explore): each preset chip plots K at that temperature on the axis and leaves a dot, so the three dots form a falling staircase; a readout shows K (two significant figures) and a small two-colour bar "more NH3 / more N2 + H2" whose split follows K's sign of log. A fourth control "Show speed" adds a second arrow label "reaches equilibrium faster" beside the 450 °C dot (qualitative, no number). Milestones: (1) 25 °C plotted, (2) 450 °C plotted (the question's case, K recorded), (3) speed label seen. QA path: chips 25, 200, 450 then "Show speed", once each, in that DOM order.
- Key state: all three dots on the axis, 450 °C last, K about 2 x 10^-4 recorded.
- Reveal draft (76 words): "Heating did not help the ammonia: K fell from about {6 × 10^5} at 25 °C to about {2 × 10^-4} at 450 °C, a factor of billions. Making ammonia releases heat, so heat pushes the balance back toward nitrogen and hydrogen. Heat still makes the reaction faster, which is why factories run hot anyway, with an iron catalyst and high pressure to win some yield back. Temperature is the one change that moves K itself."
- Phone fit: the reveal names the two K values (dots on the axis), the speed label and the falling staircase; axis, three dots, readout and speed label on screen with stage-top crop 0. Labels at least 10 px; the K axis labels alternate so they do not crowd at 390 px.
- Planned claims:
  1. N2 + 3H2 ⇌ 2NH3 has ΔH° about −92 kJ/mol and ΔS° about −198 J/(mol K) at 25 °C. support: derivation from cited values; source: CODATA / CRC standard thermodynamic properties (ΔfH NH3(g) −45.94 kJ/mol; S° NH3 192.77, N2 191.61, H2 130.68 J/(mol K)).
  2. With ΔH° and ΔS° held constant, K is about 6 x 10^5 at 25 °C, about 0.6 at 200 °C and about 2 x 10^-4 at 450 °C. support: derivation; source: ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS°, K = exp(−ΔG°/RT), node arithmetic. Assumption on the stage caption: constant ΔH° and ΔS° is an approximation; tabulated K values at high temperature differ somewhat but fall the same way (Atkins, van 't Hoff equation).
  3. For a reaction that releases heat, raising the temperature lowers K (van 't Hoff). support: derivation plus citation; source: d ln K / dT = ΔH°/(RT^2), Atkins.
  4. The industrial Haber-Bosch process runs at about 400-450 °C and about 150-300 bar with an iron catalyst, trading yield for speed. support: citation; source: Greenwood & Earnshaw, *Chemistry of the Elements*, nitrogen chapter (ammonia synthesis); or Atkins. If no source is checkable, cut the "factories" sentence from the reveal and keep claims 1-3.
- Metadata for meta.json: `also`: ["chemistry:le-chatelier", "chemistry:equilibrium-constant", "chemistry:exothermic-endothermic"]; references: none.
- curriculum: `{"id":"temperature-shifts-equilibrium","name":"Heat moves K: hotter can mean less product","level":"core","demand":2,"prerequisites":["le-chatelier","exothermic-endothermic","equilibrium-constant"]}`
- Needs interaction-designer spec: no.
- Differs from: `squeeze-the-brown-gas` shifts an equilibrium by pressure with K fixed; `four-to-one-settles` fixes K = 4; `lower-the-hill` shows a catalyst leaves the end point alone. This one changes K itself, and pits "faster" against "more".
- Safety: industrial process on the stage only.

### 12. two-routes-to-co2 · concept: hess-law · core · demand 2 · format: Single card
- Learner knows: that burning releases heat (exothermic-endothermic); that bond energies set reaction heat (pay-first-then-profit) / does not know: that the total heat of a reaction does not depend on the route, so steps add up to the direct value.
- Misconception: a reaction done in two steps releases more heat in total (two fires) or less (energy lost in between) than the same reaction done directly.
- Prediction (stage text <= 25 words): "Burn carbon straight to CO2, or first to carbon monoxide, then that to CO2. Which route releases more heat in total?" options: A. the direct route / B. the two-step route / C. both the same (answer: C, 393.51 kJ/mol both ways).
- Check (node): C + O2 → CO2, ΔH = −393.51 kJ/mol. C + ½O2 → CO, −110.53; CO + ½O2 → CO2, −282.98; sum −393.51. Detour via diamond: C(graphite) → C(diamond), +1.90; C(diamond) + O2 → CO2, −395.41; sum −393.51.
- Locked stage: an energy ladder (vertical axis, kJ/mol, 0 at "C + O2" down to −400, gridlines every 100, labels 10 px), the start level drawn, the end level hidden. Three unpressed route buttons.
- What the stage shows (explore): control 1 "Direct" draws one arrow down from the start level to CO2 with its value; control 2 "Via CO" draws two arrows (−110.53 to CO, then −282.98 to CO2) beside the first and a running-sum readout; control 3 "Via diamond" draws a small uphill arrow (+1.90) then a long downhill one (−395.41). Every route ends on the same level; the sum readout shows "−393.51" for each. Milestones: (1) direct route, (2) via-CO route with sum recorded (the question's case), (3) diamond detour. QA path: three buttons once each in DOM order; all three routes stay drawn side by side.
- Key state: three routes ending on one level, with the via-CO sum recorded.
- Reveal draft (67 words): "Every route ended on the same level: {−393.51} kJ per mole of CO2, whether you went straight down, stopped at carbon monoxide ({−110.53} then {−282.98}), or even climbed {+1.90} to diamond first. Heat released depends only on where you start and where you finish, like height lost walking downhill by any path. So chemists add measured steps to find the heat of reactions they cannot measure directly."
- Phone fit: the reveal names the three routes, the shared end level and the step values; the ladder with all three routes and the sum readout on screen with stage-top crop 0. Routes are drawn in three narrow columns (about 90 px each at 390 px) with step labels at 10 px beside the arrows.
- Planned claims:
  1. Standard enthalpy of formation of CO2(g) is −393.51 kJ/mol and of CO(g) is −110.53 kJ/mol. support: citation; source: CODATA (1989); CRC.
  2. Burning CO to CO2 releases about 282.98 kJ/mol. support: derivation; source: claim 1 by Hess's law (−393.51 − (−110.53)).
  3. Graphite to diamond takes about +1.90 kJ/mol. support: citation; source: CRC standard thermodynamic properties (ΔfH C(diamond) = 1.9 kJ/mol); NBS tables (1982) 1.895 kJ/mol.
  4. Enthalpy is a state function, so the heat of a reaction at constant pressure is the same by any route (Hess's law). support: citation plus derivation; source: Atkins, thermochemistry; the three sums above.
  5. The lesson's routes are bookkeeping: carbon burning in air makes a mixture; the stage shows each step as if it went to completion. support: model disclosure; source: the lesson.
- Metadata for meta.json: `also`: ["chemistry:exothermic-endothermic", "chemistry:allotropes"]; references: none.
- curriculum: `{"id":"hess-law","name":"Hess's law: the route doesn't change the heat","level":"core","demand":2,"prerequisites":["exothermic-endothermic","bond-energy-enthalpy"]}`
- Needs interaction-designer spec: no.
- Differs from: `pay-first-then-profit` breaks and makes bonds inside one reaction; `pencil-lead-vs-diamond` (allotropes) is structure, not energy; physics lessons on energy conservation and height do not use chemical routes. Grep for "Hess" found only `pay-first-then-profit`'s mention in passing.
- Safety: carbon monoxide appears only on the stage; no burning is suggested.

### 15. which-step-to-speed · concept: rate-determining-step · core · demand 2 · format: Single card
- Learner knows: that rate constants set how fast one step goes (multiply-dont-add-rates, the-slowing-half-life) / does not know: that in a chain of steps the slowest step sets the overall time, so speeding up a fast step barely helps.
- Misconception: making any step of a reaction faster speeds the whole reaction up in proportion.
- Prediction (stage text <= 25 words): "A → B → C. Step 1 is slow; step 2 is 100 times faster. Make step 2 ten times faster still. Half the C appears..." options: A. about 10 times sooner / B. about 2 times sooner / C. almost no sooner (answer: C, 7.03 s to 6.94 s).
- Check (node, closed form): C(t) = 1 − (k2 e^(−k1 t) − k1 e^(−k2 t)) / (k2 − k1), solved for C = 0.5 by bisection. k1 = 0.1 /s, k2 = 10 /s: t½ = 7.03 s. k2 x10 (100 /s): 6.94 s (1% sooner). k1 x10 (1 /s, k2 10 /s): 0.80 s (8.8 times sooner). Both x10: 0.70 s.
- Locked stage: two gates in series drawn as pipes (A → gate 1 → B → gate 2 → C), gate widths proportional to log k, a time axis 0-10 s with the C curve area empty, readout "half of C at: —". Controls unpressed.
- What the stage shows (explore): the curve C(t) is computed and drawn for the current k values (no animation needed; a dot marks t½). Control 1 "Speed up step 2 x10" widens gate 2 and redraws the curve: it barely moves (t½ 7.03 → 6.94 s, both shown, old curve kept as a ghost). Control 2 "Speed up step 1 x10" widens gate 1: the curve jumps left (t½ 0.70 s). Control 3 "Undo step 2" leaves only step 1 sped up (0.80 s). Milestones: (1) step 2 sped up with both t½ recorded (the question's case), (2) step 1 sped up, (3) step-1-only value seen. QA path: three buttons once each in DOM order.
- Key state: ghost curve at 7.03 s, step-2 curve at 6.94 s, step-1 curve at 0.80 s, all on one axis.
- Reveal draft (67 words): "Making the fast step ten times faster moved half-time only from {7.03} s to {6.94} s. Making the slow step ten times faster cut it to {0.80} s. In a chain, molecules queue at the narrowest gate: the slowest step sets the pace of the whole reaction. That step is the rate-determining step, and it is the one a catalyst, or a chemist, has to speed up."
- Phone fit: the reveal names the three half-times (dots on curves) and the narrowest gate; the gates, three curves and the readout on screen with stage-top crop 0. At most three curves at once (ghost, current, step-1-only), distinguished by line style before colour; labels 10 px.
- Planned claims:
  1. For consecutive first-order steps A → B → C, the amount of C is 1 − (k2 e^(−k1 t) − k1 e^(−k2 t))/(k2 − k1) of the starting A. support: derivation; source: integrating the rate equations (Atkins, consecutive elementary reactions).
  2. With k1 = 0.1 /s and k2 = 10 /s, half the C appears at about 7.0 s; with k2 = 100 /s, about 6.9 s; with k1 = 1 /s and k2 = 10 /s, about 0.8 s. support: simulation; source: the lesson's own bisection, re-run in node by the verifier.
  3. When one step is much slower than the rest, it sets the overall rate (the rate-determining step). support: citation plus derivation; source: Atkins (rate-determining step); claim 2.
  4. The rate constants are illustrative, not a real reaction. support: model disclosure; source: the lesson.
- Metadata for meta.json: `also`: ["chemistry:reaction-order", "chemistry:catalysts"]; references: none.
- curriculum: `{"id":"rate-determining-step","name":"The slowest step sets the pace","level":"core","demand":2,"prerequisites":["rate-law-concentration","reaction-order"]}`
- Needs interaction-designer spec: no.
- Differs from: `multiply-dont-add-rates` is the rate law of one step; `lower-the-hill` speeds one step with a catalyst. Run `node tools/overlap.mjs` against cs and economics (bottleneck and queue lessons): this one is a two-step chemical chain with a closed-form curve, and the brief keeps it chemical.
- Safety: abstract model only.

### 18. limestone-crossover · concept: gibbs-free-energy · stretch · demand 2 · format: Single card
- Learner knows: that some changes absorb heat (cold-pack-takes-heat); that spreading out raises entropy (why-gas-spreads) / does not know: that a change goes by itself when ΔH − TΔS is negative, so a heat-absorbing change that makes gas switches on above a definite temperature.
- Misconception: a reaction that absorbs heat can never go by itself; heating only "feeds it" gradually, with no switch point.
- Prediction (stage text <= 25 words): "Limestone (CaCO3) breaks into lime and CO2 gas, absorbing heat. Above about what temperature does it go by itself (1 bar CO2)?" options: A. about 100 °C / B. about 400 °C / C. about 850 °C / D. about 2,000 °C (answer: C, about 845 °C with 25 °C data).
- Check (node): ΔH° = −634.9 − 393.51 − (−1,207.6) = +179.2 kJ/mol; ΔS° = 38.1 + 213.79 − 91.7 = +160.2 J/(mol K). ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS°: 25 °C +131.4 kJ/mol; 500 °C +55.3; 900 °C −8.7. ΔG° = 0 at T = 179,200 / 160.2 = 1,118 K = 845 °C (holding ΔH° and ΔS° at their 25 °C values; measured decomposition at 1 bar CO2 is near 890 °C).
- Locked stage: two bars on one kJ/mol scale: "ΔH heat taken in" (fixed, 179 kJ) and "TΔS" (empty), a ΔG readout "—", and three unpressed temperature chips "25 °C / 500 °C / 900 °C". No crossover line drawn (a pre-drawn line would point at the answer).
- What the stage shows (explore): each chip sets T, grows the TΔS bar to T x 160.2 J/K and shows ΔG = ΔH − TΔS with a sign word ("does not go" / "goes by itself"). After the third chip, a fourth control "Find the switch" draws the crossover tick where the bars are equal and reads it out (845 °C). Milestones: (1) 25 °C, (2) 900 °C with ΔG recorded, (3) crossover found. QA path: chips 25, 500, 900, then "Find the switch", once each in DOM order.
- Key state: TΔS bar just past the ΔH bar at 900 °C, crossover tick at 845 °C.
- Reveal draft (76 words): "At 25 °C the TΔS bar was far shorter than the 179 kJ of heat needed, so ΔG was {+131} kJ and nothing happened. At 900 °C it passed the heat bar: ΔG was {−9} kJ and limestone breaks up by itself. The switch sits near {845} °C, where TΔS equals ΔH. Making a gas raises entropy, and temperature multiplies that gain. Absorbing heat does not forbid a change; it sets the temperature where it starts."
- Phone fit: the reveal names the two bars, the two ΔG values and the crossover tick; both bars, the readout and the tick on screen with stage-top crop 0. Labels 10 px.
- Planned claims:
  1. Standard values at 25 °C: ΔfH CaCO3 (calcite) −1,207.6, CaO −634.9, CO2 −393.51 kJ/mol; S° CaCO3 91.7, CaO 38.1, CO2 213.79 J/(mol K). support: citation; source: CRC standard thermodynamic properties; CODATA for CaO and CO2. Calcite values differ by about 1 kJ/mol between tables; the crossover moves by under 10 °C.
  2. For CaCO3 → CaO + CO2, ΔH° is about +179 kJ/mol and ΔS° about +160 J/(mol K). support: derivation; source: claim 1, node.
  3. A change at constant temperature and pressure goes by itself when ΔG = ΔH − TΔS is negative. support: citation; source: Atkins, Gibbs energy.
  4. With 25 °C values held constant, ΔG° = 0 near 845 °C; measured decomposition at 1 bar CO2 is about 890 °C. support: derivation (845 °C) plus citation (890 °C: Greenwood & Earnshaw, calcium chapter; or CRC). Caption on the stage: "estimate from 25 °C data". If the 890 °C value cannot be checked, drop it and keep "about 850 °C, an estimate".
- Metadata for meta.json: `also`: ["chemistry:entropy-mixing", "chemistry:exothermic-endothermic"]; references: none.
- curriculum: `{"id":"gibbs-free-energy","name":"ΔG = ΔH − TΔS: heat-absorbing changes switch on","level":"stretch","demand":2,"prerequisites":["exothermic-endothermic","entropy-mixing"]}`
- Needs interaction-designer spec: no.
- Differs from: `cold-pack-takes-heat` shows one endothermic change at room temperature with no ΔG; `why-gas-spreads` is entropy alone; `heat-the-ammonia-maker` (#11) moves K with temperature for an exothermic reaction. This one is the sign switch of ΔG with a single crossover temperature.
- Safety: kilns and quicklime appear only on the stage; nothing to heat at home.
