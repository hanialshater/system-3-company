### 56. calibrated-but-unequal-alarms · concept: fairness-impossibility · core · demand 3
- Format: Single card
- Audience and knowledge boundary: learner knows: false alarms and misses (flagged-means-what), that a calibrated score means "80% means 80%" (say-seventy-mean-it) / does not know: that when two groups have different base rates, one calibrated score with one threshold must give them different false-alarm and miss rates, so "fair" has to pick which equality it means.
- Misconception: If a risk score is equally accurate (calibrated) for both groups and uses one threshold, it treats both groups' innocent people the same.
- Prediction (stage text, 22 words): "A score labels people high risk (80% reoffend) or low (20%), true in both groups. Among A's non-reoffenders, the share flagged is:" options: A) the same as B's / B) higher than B's / C) lower than B's (answer: B). The locked stage shows the two 100-dot grids with their high/low labels but no outcome colours and no rates.
- Check (node, 2026-10-14): group A 100 people, 50 high, 50 low: reoffenders 40 + 10 = 50, non-reoffenders 50, false alarms (high, did not reoffend) 10, so 10/50 = 20%; misses (low, did reoffend) 10/50 = 20%. Group B 100 people, 20 high, 80 low: reoffenders 16 + 16 = 32, non-reoffenders 68, false alarms 4/68 = 5.9%, misses 16/32 = 50%. In both groups 80% of the high-labelled reoffend and 20% of the low-labelled do (calibrated by construction). Slider sweep for B's high count 10/20/30/40/50: false-alarm rate 2.7/5.9/9.7/14.3/20.0%, miss rate 69.2/50.0/36.8/27.3/20.0%; the rates match only at 50, where the base rates match (50%).
- What the stage shows: two 10x10 dot grids side by side (A left, B right), high-risk dots in the top rows with a ring. "Show outcomes" fills reoffenders dark and non-reoffenders light, and outlines the false alarms (ringed and light) in red. Under each grid, one readout line: "false alarms 10 of 50 = 20%" / "4 of 68 = 6%". A slider sets how many of B are high risk (10 to 50); grids and readouts update at once. Controls: Show outcomes, B high-risk slider, Reset (3).
- Key state (milestones): outcomes-shown (the question's case, 20% and 6% recorded), rates-equal (slider at 50, both 20%), and one other slider value seen. Reveal unlocks after outcomes-shown and rates-equal.
- Reveal payoff (draft, 82 words): "The score was honest in both groups: 8 in 10 of the ringed dots reoffended, in A and in B. Yet 20% of A's innocent people were flagged against 6% of B's, and B's misses were 50% against A's 20%. A simply has more reoffenders, so more of A sits in the high box, guilty or not. The rates only matched when you made the groups' base rates equal. With different base rates, no score is calibrated and equal on both errors."
- Phone fit (390x844): the reveal points at the ringed dots, the red false-alarm outlines and the two readout lines. Two grids at about 170 px each (cells about 15 px, dots >= 10 px), two readout lines and one slider; no table. Stage-top crop 0 px with both grids visible when the reveal opens; the slider's last value stays on the stage (the reveal's 20%/6% is quoted from the outcomes-shown milestone, so record it there).
- Planned claims:
  1. In the toy, 80% of high-labelled and 20% of low-labelled people reoffend in both groups. support: derivation (by construction).
  2. Group A: false alarms 10 of 50 non-reoffenders (20%), misses 10 of 50 reoffenders (20%). Group B: 4 of 68 (5.9%) and 16 of 32 (50%). support: derivation; source: arithmetic, node.
  3. The two groups' false-alarm and miss rates are equal only when B has 50 high-risk people, the base rate of A. support: derivation (node sweep above).
  4. A calibrated score cannot also equalise false-positive and false-negative rates across groups with different base rates, except for a perfect predictor. support: citation; source: Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan (2017), "Inherent trade-offs in the fair determination of risk scores", ITCS 2017 (arXiv 1609.05807); Chouldechova (2017), "Fair prediction with disparate impact", Big Data 5(2): 153-163. About real systems; the fact-checker can confirm the toy's case from the identity FPR = p/(1-p) * (1-PPV)/PPV * (1-FNR) given in Chouldechova (2017) eq. 2.1, with p the base rate (A: 1 * 0.25 * 0.8 = 0.2; B: 0.32/0.68 * 0.25 * 0.5 = 0.0588).
  5. (If the lesson names it, one told sentence) The 2016 COMPAS debate turned on exactly this split: one side measured calibration, the other false-positive rates. support: citation; source: Angwin et al. (2016), ProPublica, "Machine bias"; Dieterich, Mendoza and Brennan (2016), Northpointe response. About real systems; optional, cut first if the reveal is long.
- Curriculum (new concept): `{"id":"fairness-impossibility","name":"Fairness trade-off: calibrated scores give unequal error rates","level":"core","demand":3,"prerequisites":["calibration","confusion-matrix"],"subarea":"ai-society"}`; also probability:base-rates; references none; tags fairness, calibration, false positives, base rates.
- Differs from nearest: whose-cutoff-wins (training-data-bias) has a model that is wrong for the rare group; here the score is right for both and the unequal errors come from base rates alone. say-seventy-mean-it teaches calibration in one group. flagged-means-what reads one confusion matrix. probability base-rate lessons have no second group and no fairness choice.

### 57. asthma-looks-safe · concept: prediction-not-intervention · core · demand 3
- Format: Before / after toggle
- Audience and knowledge boundary: learner knows: a model learns patterns from past records / does not know: that records carry the effects of past decisions, so a model that predicts well can give the wrong answer to "what should we do?", and acting on it removes the very care that made the pattern.
- Misconception: If a model trained on hospital records predicts accurately that a group dies less, that group is low risk and can safely get less care.
- Prediction (stage text, 24 words; the stage caption says these are pneumonia patients): "In past records every asthma patient went to intensive care. Now the model sends low-risk patients to the ward. Asthma patients' death rate becomes:" options: A) about 4%, as before / B) about 8%, like others / C) about 20% (answer: C). The locked stage shows the 200 patient dots, asthma dots marked, with no ICU marks and no death counts.
- Check (node, 2026-10-14; deterministic expected counts, no sampling): 50 asthma patients, 150 others. Toy risks: ward care, asthma 20%, others 8%; intensive care cuts asthma risk to 4%. Old rule (asthma -> ICU): asthma deaths 50 x 0.04 = 2 (4%), others 150 x 0.08 = 12 (8%), total 14. A model fitted to these records scores asthma 4% < 8%, so asthma is its lowest-risk group. Model rule (lowest risk -> ward): asthma deaths 50 x 0.20 = 10 (20%), others unchanged at 12, total 22.
- What the stage shows: one panel of 200 dots (50 asthma, marked with a small "A"); a care strip shows which dots are in the ICU box. The toggle "who decides care: old rule / model" moves the asthma dots between the ICU box and the ward; dots that die turn dark (exact counts, fixed which dots). One readout line: "asthma died 2 of 50 (4%) · others 12 of 150 (8%)". A "model's view" chip shows the risk ranking the model learned from the old records (asthma lowest). Controls: model's view, care toggle, Reset (3).
- Key state (milestones): model-view-seen (asthma ranked lowest, 4% vs 8% recorded), model-decides (toggle to model; asthma 10 of 50 = 20% and total 22 recorded). Reveal unlocks after both. The old rule's numbers stay as a faint "before" row under the readout.
- Reveal payoff (draft, 79 words): "The records were right: asthma patients died less, 2 of 50 against 12 of 150. But they died less because every one of them went to intensive care. The model saw the outcome, not the reason, and ranked asthma as the safest group. When its ranking chose the care, asthma patients went to the ward and 10 of 50 died. A model that predicts well answers 'who did badly before?', not 'what happens if we change what we do?'"
- Phone fit (390x844): the reveal points at the asthma dots, the ICU box (empty of asthma after the toggle), the before row and the after readout. One 200-dot panel (20 x 10, dots >= 10 px) above a one-line readout and a faint before row; no table. Stage-top crop 0 px with the panel and both rows visible when the reveal opens.
- Planned claims:
  1. Under the old rule, 2 of 50 asthma patients (4%) and 12 of 150 others (8%) die. support: derivation (toy risks stated on the stage: ward 20% and 8%, ICU 4%).
  2. A model fitted to those records ranks asthma as the lowest-risk group. support: derivation (4% < 8%; the toy model is the group death rate).
  3. When the model's ranking sends asthma patients to the ward, 10 of 50 (20%) die and total deaths rise from 14 to 22. support: derivation; source: arithmetic, node.
  4. A pneumonia-risk model trained on real hospital data learned that asthma lowers the risk of death, because asthmatic patients had been admitted directly to intensive care. support: citation; source: Caruana, Lou, Gehrke, Koch, Sturm and Elhadad (2015), "Intelligible models for healthcare: predicting pneumonia risk and hospital 30-day readmission", KDD 2015, section on the asthma pattern (the original rule-learning result is Cooper et al. 1997, Artificial Intelligence in Medicine 9(2)). About real systems; the toy's 4%, 8% and 20% are invented and the lesson says so.
- Curriculum (new concept): `{"id":"prediction-not-intervention","name":"Prediction is not intervention: records carry past decisions","level":"core","demand":3,"prerequisites":["probability:confounding"],"subarea":"ai-society"}`; also probability:confounding; references none; tags causation, healthcare, deployment.
- Differs from nearest: patrol-feedback-loop (feedback-loops) is about the model's own decisions creating its next data; here the trap is in the first model, inherited from a human policy. background-shortcut (shortcut-learning) is a spurious cue that breaks on new images; here the cue is real and the prediction stays accurate until it is used to act. The probability confounding lessons show a hidden third factor; this one adds the deployment step that makes the confounder deadly.

### 67. zero-crashes-in-a-thousand · concept: zero-failure-testing · core · demand 2
- Format: Simulation run
- Strand: ai-society
- Audience and knowledge boundary: learner knows: chance results vary from run to run (probability sample-size-noise), the chance of at least one event in many tries (probability at-least-once) / does not know: that zero failures in n tests only bounds the failure rate at about 3/n with 95% confidence (the rule of three), so showing an AI system is rarely unsafe takes enormous numbers of tests.
- Misconception: If an AI system passes 1,000 tests with zero failures, its failure rate must be below 1 in 1,000.
- Prediction (stage text, 25 words): "A car drives 1,000 test trips with zero crashes. Which true crash rate would still pass this test about 1 time in 20?" options: A) 0.1 in 1,000 / B) 1 in 1,000 / C) 3 in 1,000 (answer: C). The locked stage shows an empty grid of 100 test cards and an empty "passed" tally.
- Check (node, 2026-10-14): chance of zero crashes in 1,000 trips = (1 - r)^1000: r = 0.1, 1, 2, 3 per 1,000 -> 90.5%, 36.8%, 13.5%, 4.96%. 95% upper bound with zero failures: 1 - 0.05^(1/1000) = 2.99 per 1,000 (rule of three: 3/n). To bound a rate below 1 in a million: about 3.0 million failure-free trips (ln 0.05 / ln(1 - 1e-6) = 2,995,731). Simulation (mulberry32 seed 42, each of 100 tests is 1,000 trips, one random number per trip, crash if below r): passes 36 at r = 1, 14 at r = 2, 3 at r = 3 per 1,000; over 20,000 tests the pass shares are 36.4%, 13.3%, 4.84%.
- What the stage shows: 100 small test cards in a 10x10 grid; each card turns green (zero crashes) or shows its crash count. Preset buttons in DOM order: "100 tests at 3 per 1,000", "100 tests at 1 per 1,000", "Next seed", Reset (4). Each preset runs instantly with the seed shown (42 first; "Next seed" re-runs the current rate with seed + 1). Readout: "passed 3 of 100 · true rate 3 per 1,000 · seed 42".
- Key state (milestones): rate3-run (pass count at 3 per 1,000 recorded, with seed), rate1-run (pass count at 1 per 1,000 recorded), reseeded. Reveal unlocks after rate3-run and rate1-run.
- Reveal payoff (draft, 78 words, numbers from the learner's runs; seed-42 values shown): "A car that crashes 3 times per 1,000 trips still passed the zero-crash test in 3 of your 100 tests; the expected share is 5%, 1 in 20. At 1 per 1,000 it passed 36. So zero crashes in 1,000 trips only shows the rate is probably below about 3 per 1,000: the rule of three. Showing a rate below 1 in a million would need about 3 million crash-free trips."
- Phone fit (390x844): the reveal points at the green cards and the readout. 10x10 grid of 30 px cards (about 330 px square is too tall with the reveal): draw cards at 18 px with 4 px gaps (about 220 px), crash counts hidden at that size (colour plus a dot for "crashed"), one readout line. Stage-top crop 0 px with grid and readout visible when the reveal opens; the rate-3 result stays as a faint "at 3 per 1,000: 3 passed" row after the rate-1 run.
- Planned claims:
  1. The chance that 1,000 independent trips with crash rate r have zero crashes is (1 - r)^1000: 36.8% at 1 per 1,000 and 5.0% at 3 per 1,000. support: derivation; source: node.
  2. With zero failures in n trials, the 95% upper confidence bound on the failure rate is about 3/n (here 2.99 per 1,000). support: derivation; source: Hanley and Lippman-Hand (1983), "If nothing goes wrong, is everything all right? Interpreting zero numerators", JAMA 249(13): 1743-1745.
  3. In the seeded run (mulberry32, seed 42, disclosed), 3 of 100 tests pass at 3 per 1,000 and 36 at 1 per 1,000. support: simulation; source: node, the lesson's own generator.
  4. Demonstrating autonomous-vehicle safety by test driving alone would take hundreds of millions of miles (about 275 million failure-free miles to show a fatality rate below the US human rate with 95% confidence). support: citation; source: Kalra and Paddock (2016), "Driving to safety: how many miles of driving would it take to demonstrate autonomous vehicle reliability?", Transportation Research Part A 94: 182-193 (RAND RR-1478). About real systems; optional told sentence, cut first (not in the reveal draft).
- Curriculum (new concept): `{"id":"zero-failure-testing","name":"Zero failures in testing: the rule of three","level":"core","demand":2,"prerequisites":["probability:at-least-once"],"subarea":"ai-society"}`; also probability:confidence-intervals; references none; tags safety, testing, evaluation.
- Differs from nearest: the probability at-least-once lessons compute the chance of one or more events at a known rate; this runs it backwards from an observed zero to a bound on the rate, in a safety setting. best-of-twenty-on-one-test (test-set-reuse) is about scores flattered by selection; this is about how little a clean record proves.
