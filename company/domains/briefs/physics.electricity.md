### 1. metal-feels-colder · concept: heat-flow-feels-cold · intro · demand 3 · format: Single card
- Learner knows: hot and cold; a thermometer reads temperature; metal feels cold to touch. / does not know: that objects left in one room reach the same temperature, and that skin senses the rate heat leaves it (set by the material's conductivity and heat capacity), not the object's temperature.
- Misconception: Metal in a room is colder than wood in the same room.
- Prediction (stage text ≤ 25 words): "A metal block and a wooden block have sat in the same 20 °C room all day. Which one is colder?" options: A. The metal / B. The wood / C. Neither: both 20 °C (answer: C). The question names no conductivity and no "feels", so the locked stage does not point at the answer; all three options are equally plain.
- Check: both blocks are at room temperature (thermal equilibrium). Contact temperature of two semi-infinite bodies, Tc = (e1 T1 + e2 T2) / (e1 + e2) with effusivity e = sqrt(k rho c). Skin 33 °C, e ≈ 1,176; pine e ≈ 310; aluminium e ≈ 24,000 (W s^0.5 m^-2 K^-1). Re-run: Tc(wood) = 30.3 °C, Tc(aluminium) = 20.6 °C; finite-difference simulation gives the same 30.3 / 20.6. Heat-flux ratio aluminium / wood at equal time = 4.6. With skin e = 1,800 instead: 31.1 / 20.9 (the result is robust to the skin value).
- What the stage shows: two blocks side by side on one table (one picture, not two panels). Control 1: a thermometer probe chip, tap a block to read it (both read 20 °C). Control 2: a fingertip chip, tap a block to touch it; the fingertip shows its skin temperature readout and a heat-flow arrow from finger to block whose thickness is the flux. Control 3: material chip for the "metal" block (aluminium / steel) so the learner sees the effect is the material, not the label. ≤ 6 controls, all present from first render.
- Key state (milestones): (1) thermometer read on both blocks, (2) finger touched the metal, (3) finger touched the wood. The reveal unlocks only after all three. Record the readouts when each milestone fires so Reset cannot erase them; keep a two-row "Seen" line (thermometer 20 / 20; skin 21 / 30) on the stage under the picture, so the quoted values are visible when the reveal opens.
- Reveal payoff (draft, 73 words): "Your thermometer read 20 °C on both blocks. Yet when the finger touched, its skin dropped to about 21 °C on the metal and only to about 30 °C on the wood, and the metal's heat-flow arrow was over four times thicker. Metal conducts heat away from your skin fast, so the skin cools; wood barely takes any, so the skin stays warm. Your finger senses heat leaving it, not the object's temperature."
- Phone fit: at 390×844 the blocks, the fingertip readout and the Seen line must be on screen with the reveal's last line (stage-top crop 0 px). The picture is one row (two blocks + finger); fold the readouts into labels on the blocks rather than a table.
- Planned claims:
  1. Objects left in the same room for a long time reach the same temperature (thermal equilibrium, zeroth law). support: textbook; source: any introductory thermodynamics text (e.g. Halliday, Resnick and Walker, *Fundamentals of Physics*, chapter on temperature).
  2. When two bodies touch, the contact temperature is Tc = (e1 T1 + e2 T2)/(e1 + e2), e = sqrt(k rho c) (semi-infinite bodies, perfect contact). support: derivation; source: Carslaw and Jaeger, *Conduction of Heat in Solids* (2nd ed., 1959), two semi-infinite solids in contact; re-derived and simulated in `artifacts/scratch/cm-physics-r3/check.mjs`.
  3. With skin at 33 °C, contact temperature is about 21 °C on aluminium and about 30 °C on pine at 20 °C. support: derivation + simulation; source: check.mjs. Property values (aluminium k 237, rho 2,700, c 900; pine k 0.12, rho 500, c 1,600; skin k 0.37, rho 1,100, c 3,400) from a heat-transfer table (Incropera et al., *Fundamentals of Heat and Mass Transfer*, appendix A); skin and wood values vary and are labelled illustrative.
  4. Heat flow from the finger into aluminium is about 4.6 times that into pine at the same moment (flux ∝ e_s e_o/(e_s + e_o)). support: derivation; source: check.mjs. Reveal says "over four times".
  5. Skin thermoreceptors respond to skin temperature and its change, so touch reports heat loss rather than the object's temperature. support: textbook; source: any physiology text on thermoreception (fact-checker picks the edition); the lesson does not name receptor types.
  6. Idealisations stated on the stage caption or in assumptions: perfect contact, semi-infinite blocks, no blood flow over the first seconds.

### 2. bird-on-the-wire · concept: potential-difference · core · demand 3 · format: Single card
- Learner knows: current flows round a loop (current-same-around-loop); high voltage is dangerous. / does not know: that voltage is a difference between two points, and current flows through something only when its two ends sit at different voltages.
- Misconception: A bird on an 11,000 V line has 11,000 V through it and survives only because birds are insulated or lucky.
- Prediction (stage text ≤ 25 words): "A bird stands on one bare power line at 11,000 V. What voltage is across its two feet?" options: A. About 11,000 V / B. About 5,500 V / C. Less than 1 V (answer: C). No option is more exact than the others.
- Check: line current 200 A, conductor resistance 0.2 Ω/km (illustrative overhead conductor), feet 5 cm apart. Re-run: wire segment 1.0e-5 Ω, drop 200 × 1.0e-5 = 0.002 V (2 mV); with an illustrative bird resistance of 10 kΩ, about 0.2 µA flows through the bird. Wing on the grounded pole: 11,000 V across the bird.
- What the stage shows: one side view, a line, a pole and a bird. Control 1: a voltmeter probe the learner taps onto foot 1, foot 2, then "between the feet" (readouts 11,000 V, 11,000 V, 0.002 V). Control 2: a foot-spacing slider (2 to 20 cm) showing the drop stays in thousandths of a volt. Control 3: a "wing on the pole" toggle that bridges line and ground; the difference readout jumps to 11,000 V and a current arrow through the bird appears. Current through the bird is drawn as an arrow whose thickness follows the readout, so the cause (difference, not level) is on the stage.
- Key state (milestones): (1) both feet read 11,000 V, (2) between-feet read under 1 V, (3) wing-on-pole difference seen. Record each reading when its milestone fires; keep a one-line Seen row ("each foot 11,000 V · across feet 0.002 V · to pole 11,000 V").
- Reveal payoff (draft, 80 words): "Your voltmeter read 11,000 V at each foot, and only 0.002 V between them: the 5 cm of wire between the feet drops just 2 thousandths of a volt even with 200 A flowing. Current needs a voltage difference to push it, so almost none goes through the bird. When you put a wing on the grounded pole, the difference jumped to 11,000 V. Voltage is always between two points. Never approach real power lines: current can jump across air."
- Phone fit: the line, the bird and the pole sit in one band across the top; the Seen row folds into the voltmeter label. Stage-top crop 0 px at 390×844 with the reveal open.
- Planned claims:
  1. Current through a conductor needs a potential difference across it, I = ΔV / R (Ohm's law). support: textbook; source: any introductory electricity text.
  2. A 5 cm length of conductor at 0.2 Ω/km carrying 200 A drops 0.002 V. support: derivation (V = I R L); source: check.mjs. Current and resistance labelled illustrative; real line currents and conductors vary.
  3. A bird touching only one conductor has almost no current through it; touching a grounded pole or a second conductor puts the full line-to-ground or line-to-line voltage across it, which kills birds. support: derivation + citation; source: derivation from claim 1; electrocution of birds bridging conductors and poles is documented in APLIC, *Suggested Practices for Avian Protection on Power Lines* (2006) (fact-checker confirms).
  4. High voltages can arc across an air gap, so approaching a line without touching it is dangerous. support: citation; source: the breakdown field of air is about 3 MV/m (any electrostatics text); the safety wording goes past a reader other than the author (W-361 precedent).
  5. Idealisation stated: the line is drawn as a steady (DC-like) voltage; on an AC line both feet rise and fall together, so the difference stays tiny. support: derivation.
