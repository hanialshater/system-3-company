### 1. contact-order-matters · concept: contact-timing-concurrency (new, core, demand 2; prerequisite herd-immunity) · core · demand 2
- curriculum.references: easley-kleinberg-2010 ch. 21 §21.6 (§ unconfirmed)
- Learner knows: that infection spreads along contacts; a "who is connected to whom" diagram / does not know: that infection can only move forward in time, so the same contacts in another order reach different people.
- Misconception: "If you can trace a chain of contacts from the first case to someone, that person is at risk." (A contact diagram ignores when each contact happened.)
- Prediction (stage shows only the four contacts as a diagram with no dates, A infected): "A is infected. Four contacts happen one at a time: A-B, B-C, C-D, D-E. If they happen in the order D-E, C-D, B-C, A-B, how many people other than A can get infected?" options: A. 0 / B. 1 / C. 4 (answer: B). Check (node, rule below): reversed order reaches only B; the order A-B, B-C, C-D, D-E reaches B, C, D, E (4). Rule: contacts happen one at a time; a person can pass the infection on only at a contact that comes after they were infected; every such contact transmits (a deliberately certain toy rule). The answer is not visible before the learner commits: the stage shows an undated diagram, and the order is in the question.
- Interaction (storyboard / scrub; matches `company/design/specs/contact-order-matters.md`): a dated contact timeline, rows A to E, days 0 to 4, four contacts as links. Controls in DOM order (7): Day slider, "Day 4" button, order chips "A→E", "E→A" (start, the question's own order), "Overlap", and person buttons "A" and "E" that choose the first case (start A) and keep the day and the order. People turn from healthy to infected only as the cursor passes a contact (on a day after the carrier was infected). The reveal quotes only what the learner can reach: E→A from A reaches 1 (B, day 4); A→E from A reaches 4; Overlap reaches 4 from A and 4 from E (checked: from E, D, C, B, A, one per day). From E, A→E reaches only D, quotable only if reached. The 1/k! result and "12 of 24 orders reach one person" are NOT reachable on the stage and are not in the reveal as seen. One representation: the dated contact timeline. Neutral "contacts", no disease named and no health advice.
- Payoff: The same contacts can protect or expose someone depending on their order; overlapping contacts open paths in both directions. A diagram of who knows whom overstates who can be reached (Morris and Kretzschmar 1997 on concurrency; unverified).
- Node check (re-run 2026-10-14 by me, own function `reach`, earliest-infection-time relaxation): (a) 3 people, A infected, A-B in March then B-C in February: reaches B only; B-C moved to April: reaches B and C (matches the ask). (b) 4 contacts on the path A-B-C-D-E, all 24 orders, A infected: reach counts are 1 person in 12 orders, 2 in 8, 3 in 3, 4 in 1 (mean 1.708). Closed form: P(reach at least k) = 1/k!, giving 24/24, 12/24, 4/24, 1/24, which agrees. (c) Overlap, all four contacts spanning the same period: reach 4 from A and 4 from E. (d) Serial A-B (months 1-2) then B-C (3-4): from A reaches B, C; from C reaches B only. Reversed serial: from A reaches B only; from C reaches A and B. Order alone makes the paths one-way; overlap makes them two-way (B infected in month 1 can still meet C in month 2-3). Only the answer (reversed = 1) is used in the prediction; the 1/k! result is a note only: the stage cannot reach it, so it is not a learner-facing claim unless told as arithmetic with its own claim, never as something the learner saw.
- Planned claims: (1) In the toy rule, infection reaches a person only through contacts that come after the carrier was infected (convention, the lesson's rule). (2) In the forward order all four people are reached; in the reversed order only B is (simulation / arithmetic from the rule, rerunnable). (3) Overlap rule (spec): days 1 to 4, the first case is infected on day 0, and every pair meets on each of days 1 to 4; on day d a contact infects the other person only if one of the pair was infected on an earlier day (no relay on the same day). Under this rule infection reaches all four from either end, one person per day (simulation, rerunnable; re-run 2026-10-14: from A days 1,2,3,4 for B..E; from E days 1,2,3,4 for D..A). (4) Concurrent partnerships can raise reach compared with the same partnerships in sequence (Morris and Kretzschmar 1997, "Concurrent partnerships and the spread of HIV", AIDS 11(5); **unverified, needs a human source check by the fact-checker, including the citation details**). (5) Real transmission is not certain at each contact and contacts are not known in advance (convention).
- Format: Storyboard / scrub (`company/design/formats.md`; by hand with a slider today; a kit `<Timeline>` is proposed). Spec written (interaction designer, draft, in design review): chips, scrubber and two person buttons on a 390 px phone (labels not clipping, tap targets 44 px). Illustrations: none.
- Overlap: nearest lessons are `herd-immunity`, `pockets-below-threshold` and `spreaders-are-rare` (who gets infected, random mixing or clusters, with no time order) and `waves-return` (time, but population-level waves). Differs: this is about the order of individual contacts on a fixed set of links. `node tools/overlap.mjs` scans lesson metadata only; this brief is not yet in it. No existing lesson teaches temporal paths.

### 1. outbreak-can-fizzle-above-one · concept: life:branching-extinction · core · demand 3 · rank 17 · format: Simulation run (seeded outbreaks, R chips)
- Reference: Easley & Kleinberg 2010, ch. 21.
- Audience: students in a networks course and curious adults. Learner knows: R above 1 means growth on average (immunity-eats-r) / does not know: that with R = 1.5 (Poisson spread), about 42% of single introductions die out by chance.
- Misconception: If R is above 1, every introduced case starts an epidemic.
- Prediction (stage text ≤ 25 words): "Each case infects a random number of others, 1.5 on average. One case arrives. How often does the chain die out by itself?" options: A. Never / B. About 4 in 10 / C. About 9 in 10 (answer: B, 41.7%).
- Check: Poisson offspring. Node: `{"R1_5":0.417,"R2":0.203,"R3":0.06,"R0_9":1,"sim_R1_5_diedOut":0.429}` (10,000 seeded chains, seed 218; chains past 2,000 cases counted as taking off).
- Reveal (one line): The first case often infects 0 or 1 by luck (56% of the time), so 42% of chains fizzle; R above 1 means growth is possible, not certain.
- Planned claims:
  1. With Poisson spread and mean 1.5, the chance that one case's chain dies out is 41.7% (solution of q = e^{1.5(q − 1)}). support: derivation; source: node fixed point.
  2. The chance is 20.3% for R = 2 and 6.0% for R = 3; for R below 1 every chain dies out. support: derivation; source: node.
  3. In 10,000 simulated chains with R = 1.5, 42.9% died out. support: simulation; source: node seed 218.
- curriculum: `{"primary":"life:branching-extinction","also":["probability:at-least-once"],"prerequisites":["life:reproduction-number-growth"],"level":"core","strand":"health-epidemiology","references":[{"work":"easley-kleinberg-2010","chapter":21}],"tags":["epidemics","branching process"]}`
- Needs interaction-designer spec: no.
- Differs from nearest: spreaders-are-rare counts cases that infect no one (dispersion); immunity-eats-r is the average R; herd-immunity shows early die-out only below the threshold. This asks for the whole chain's survival chance at R above 1.

### 2. early-fatality-count · concept: life:case-fatality-lag · core · demand 3 · rank 1 · format: Single card (day slider drives one timeline and a ratio readout)
- Why this one next: demand 3 and the category focus. "Deaths so far ÷ cases so far" is the number people quote early in every outbreak, and it is biased in a way a slider shows in seconds.
- Audience / knowledge boundary: knows doubling time (`three-day-doubling`) and that reports lag (`stale-case-counts`). Does not know that a ratio of two counts, one of them lagged, is pulled far below the true share while cases grow.
- Misconception: "Deaths so far divided by cases so far is the share of cases that die."
- Prediction (stage text, 19 words): "Made-up outbreak: cases double every 5 days. Exactly 2 in 100 cases die, always 15 days after being counted." Question: "On day 45, with cases still rising, what is deaths so far ÷ cases so far?" options: A. About 1 in 50 / B. About 1 in 100 / C. About 1 in 400 (answer: C, 1 in 405). The locked stage shows the cases curve to day 45 only, with no ratio readout.
- Model: daily cases 10·2^(d/5) for days 0 to 60, then halving every 5 days to day 120, none after; exactly 2% of each day's cases die 15 days later; every case is counted. Deterministic, no seed.
- What the stage shows: one timeline, days 0 to 135: daily cases as bars and daily deaths as dots in the same panel (deaths drawn ×50 so a 2% share would line up; the axis label says so). Controls (1): day slider. Readout above the chart: "deaths so far ÷ cases so far: 1 in N".
- Key state: the learner has scrubbed past day 120, so the readout has gone from about 1 in 400 to 1 in 50. Reveal unlocks after day 120.
- Check (node, re-run 2026-10-14): ratio on day 30: 0.226% (1 in 442); day 45: 0.247% (1 in 405); day 60: 0.250% (1 in 400); day 75: 1.135% (1 in 88); day 90: 1.897% (1 in 53); day 120: 1.998%; day 135: 2.000% (1 in 50). While cases grow, ratio → 0.02 × 2^(−15/5) = 0.0025.
- Reveal draft (≤ 90 words; 67 words): "On day 45 the readout said about 1 in 400, though 1 in 50 cases die. Today's deaths come from cases counted 15 days earlier, when there were 8 times fewer (three doublings). Once cases fell, the ratio climbed and ended at 1 in 50. While cases grow, deaths so far ÷ cases so far runs low. Toy rule: a fixed 15-day delay and every case counted."
- Phone fit (390×844): the readout and the timeline are one panel at the stage top; the slider is the only control row. The reveal points at the readout and the curve, both inside that panel.
- Planned claims:
  1. In the model, on day 45 deaths so far ÷ cases so far is 0.247%, about 1 in 405; on day 135 it is exactly 2%. support: arithmetic / simulation; source: node, deterministic, rerunnable.
  2. While cases double every 5 days and deaths follow 15 days later, the ratio tends to 2% × 2^(−3) = 0.25%: today's deaths come from a case count 8 times smaller. support: derivation.
  3. The 2%, the 5-day doubling and the fixed 15-day delay are invented; real delays vary between people, and real case counts miss cases (convention, stated on the card).
  4. Optional context for the fact-checker, not on the card unless verified: the delay between case and death biases early naive fatality estimates (Ghani et al. 2005, "Methods for estimating the case fatality ratio for a novel, emerging infectious disease", Am. J. Epidemiol. 162(5)). **Unverified, needs a human source check including the citation.** The lesson stands on claims 1 to 3.
- curriculum: `{"primary":"life:case-fatality-lag","also":["life:reading-case-curves","life:outbreak-doubling-time"],"prerequisites":["life:outbreak-doubling-time"],"level":"core","strand":"health-epidemiology","tags":["epidemics","delay","ratios"]}`
- Needs interaction-designer spec: no (Single card, one slider).
- Differs from nearest: `stale-case-counts` is one lagged curve (reported vs infected today); this is a ratio of two counts where only one lags. `falling-yet-adding` is about totals after a peak. `most-cases-vaccinated` is a different ratio trap (base rates). No existing lesson teaches the bias in a running ratio.

### 3. screening-survival-clock · concept: life:lead-time-survival · core · demand 3 · rank 2 · format: Single card (test toggle + lead slider, 14 timeline rows)
- Why this one next: demand 3. "5-year survival went up" is a widely quoted health number, and lead time is a misconception that a stage can show directly: the death marks do not move while the survival count rises.
- Audience / knowledge boundary: knows what "alive 5 years after diagnosis" means. Does not know that the clock starts at diagnosis, so finding an illness earlier raises this count even when nobody lives a day longer.
- Misconception: "If more people are alive 5 years after diagnosis, they must be living longer."
- Prediction (stage text, 21 words): "14 people with a made-up illness. It is found when symptoms start, and each person dies 1 to 8 years later." Question: "A new test finds it 3 years earlier. Treatment changes nothing: every death stays on the same day. What share is alive 5 years after diagnosis?" options: A. 43% / B. 64% / C. 86% (answer: C, 12 of 14). Today's share is 43% (6 of 14), and it is shown on the locked stage as the starting readout. All options are numbers.
- Model: times from symptom diagnosis to death are 1.25, 1.75, ..., 7.75 years (14 values, every half year). The test moves each diagnosis earlier by the lead time L; death dates are fixed. Alive at 5 years ⇔ T + L > 5. Deterministic.
- What the stage shows: 14 rows on one shared time axis. Each row has a diagnosis dot, a death cross and a 5-year bracket starting at the dot; a row turns solid when its death is past the bracket end. Controls (2): toggle "Test: off / on" and lead slider 0 to 3 years (step 0.5, active when the test is on, starts at 3). Readout: "alive 5 years after diagnosis: k of 14".
- Key state: the learner has switched the test on at 3 years and seen 12 of 14, with the crosses unmoved, and then moved the slider at least once. Reveal unlocks after that.
- Check (node, re-run 2026-10-14): L = 0: 6/14 (42.9%); 0.5: 7; 1: 8; 1.5: 9; 2: 10; 2.5: 11; 3: 12/14 (85.7%). Deaths identical in every case. Option B (64%, 9 of 14) is the 1.5-year value, a fair distractor.
- Reveal draft (≤ 90 words; 64 words): "With the test on, 12 of 14 were alive 5 years after diagnosis, up from 6, yet every death cross stayed put. Only the start of the clock moved. Finding an illness earlier raises survival counted from diagnosis even when nobody lives longer: lead time. To judge an earlier test, compare deaths in whole groups. This toy makes no claim about any real test."
- Phone fit (390×844): 14 rows at ≥ 18 px fit in the top half of the stage; the readout sits above the rows and the controls in one row below. The reveal points at the crosses and the readout.
- Planned claims:
  1. In the model, with no test 6 of 14 (43%) are alive 5 years after diagnosis; with a 3-year lead, 12 of 14 (86%), with every death on the same day. support: arithmetic; source: node, rerunnable (count T + L > 5).
  2. Survival counted from diagnosis rises by construction when diagnosis moves earlier and death does not (definition of lead time). support: derivation.
  3. Comparing deaths in whole groups (such as a trial of tested vs untested people) is not affected by when diagnosis happens. support: derivation from the model (death dates do not depend on the test). Optional context, not on the card unless verified: Welch, Schwartz and Woloshin 2000, "Are increasing 5-year survival rates evidence of success against cancer?", JAMA 283(22). **Unverified, needs a human source check including the citation.**
  4. The 14 people, the 1 to 8 year spread and "treatment changes nothing" are invented; the lesson says nothing about any real test or illness (convention, stated on the card). No advice about screening.
- curriculum: `{"primary":"life:lead-time-survival","also":[],"prerequisites":[],"level":"core","strand":"health-epidemiology","tags":["screening","survival statistics","bias"]}`
- Needs interaction-designer spec: no (Single card, two controls). If the toggle and slider are judged two controls of one change, the builder may merge them into the slider alone (0 = test off).
- Differs from nearest: `probability/base-rates` is about what a positive test result means (predictive value), not about survival after diagnosis. `stale-case-counts` and `early-fatality-count` are delays in outbreak counts. No lesson in any category teaches lead time.
