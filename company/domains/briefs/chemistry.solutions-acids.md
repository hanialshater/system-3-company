### 3. find-the-co2-in-air · concept: parts-per-million · intro · demand 3 · format: Single card
- Learner knows: what a percentage is; that air is a mixture of gases; that a drop holds about 10^21 molecules (molecules-in-one-drop) / does not know: what a share of a few parts per million looks like, and that a tiny share can still be an enormous count.
- Misconception: carbon dioxide is a sizeable part of air (several per cent or more), and "a few parts per million" means "almost none there".
- Prediction (stage text <= 25 words): "Take 10,000 molecules of dry air. About how many are carbon dioxide?" options: A. about 4 / B. about 40 / C. about 400 / D. about 2,000 (answer: A; 420 ppm = 4.2 per 10,000).
- Check (node): dry air N2 780,840 ppm, O2 209,460 ppm, Ar 9,340 ppm, CO2 about 420 ppm (2023) -> per 10,000: 7,808.4, 2,094.6, 93.4, 4.2. Drawn as 7,808 + 2,095 + 93 + 4 = 10,000; the remaining gases (Ne, He, CH4, Kr, H2 and others, about 27 ppm together, 0.27 per 10,000) round to no dot and are disclosed. One breath (0.5 L, 25 °C, 1 atm): n = PV/RT = 101,325 x 0.0005 / (8.314 x 298.15) = 0.0204 mol, x 6.022e23 x 420e-6 = 5.2e18 CO2 molecules.
- Locked stage: a 100 x 100 grid of 10,000 identical grey dots (texture, not tappable) and the caption "10,000 molecules of dry air". No colours yet.
- What the stage shows (explore): four gas chips (N2, O2, Ar, CO2); tapping a chip colours that gas's dots in place (fixed, disclosed layout, seeded positions via mulberry32) and adds its count to one readout row. CO2's 4 dots are too small to find in the grid, so tapping CO2 also rings them and a "zoom x10" step button magnifies around one ring until a single CO2 molecule fills a cell. Last control "one breath" swaps the grid caption to a count readout: "one breath, about 0.5 L: about 5 x 10^18 CO2 molecules". Milestones: (1) CO2 chip tapped with its count shown, (2) zoomed to one CO2 dot, (3) breath count shown.
- Key state: all four counts on the readout row (recorded as the learner taps), the 4 CO2 dots ringed.
- Reveal draft (71 words): "Of your 10,000 dots, {7,808} were nitrogen, {2,095} oxygen, {93} argon and only {4} carbon dioxide: about 420 parts per million, or 0.042%. A part per million is a share, not an amount. One breath of about half a litre still holds about 5 × 10^18 CO2 molecules. A tiny share of a huge crowd is still a huge number, which is why trace gases are counted in ppm, not per cent."
- Phone fit: the reveal names the four counts and the breath count; the grid (top on screen, CO2 rings visible), the counts row and the breath readout must all fit with stage-top crop 0. The grid is at most about 300 px square on a 390 px phone (3 px dots are fine as untapped texture; the rings carry the CO2 dots at a visible size).
- Planned claims:
  1. Dry air is about 78.08% nitrogen, 20.95% oxygen and 0.93% argon by volume (molecule count). support: citation; source: CRC, "Composition of dry air" / U.S. Standard Atmosphere 1976.
  2. Carbon dioxide was about 420 ppm of the atmosphere in 2023 (global mean). support: citation; source: WMO Greenhouse Gas Bulletin No. 20 (October 2024), 420.0 ppm for 2023; cross-check NOAA GML or Scripps Mauna Loa records if reachable. The lesson says "about 420 ppm", never a single year's decimals.
  3. 420 ppm is 4.2 per 10,000, 0.042%; the grid's 7,808 + 2,095 + 93 + 4 rounds each share and leaves out gases under 1 per 10,000 (about 27 ppm in total). support: derivation; source: node arithmetic.
  4. One breath of about 0.5 L of air at 25 °C and 1 atm holds about 5 x 10^18 CO2 molecules. support: derivation; source: ideal gas law with R = 8.314 J/(mol K) and NA (SI 2019 exact); tidal volume about 0.5 L at rest (Guyton & Hall, *Textbook of Medical Physiology*, pulmonary ventilation).
  5. The dot positions are a seeded layout (mulberry32, seed disclosed), not a sample of real air. support: model disclosure; source: the lesson.
- Metadata for meta.json: `also`: ["chemistry:atom-scale-size", "chemistry:ideal-gas-law"]; references: none.
- curriculum: `{"id":"parts-per-million","name":"Parts per million: tiny shares, huge counts","level":"intro","demand":3,"prerequisites":["dilution","moles-counting"]}`
- Needs interaction-designer spec: no. Watch the format rule on large dot fields: the 10,000 dots are untapped texture; only chips and buttons are controls.
- Differs from: `molecules-in-one-drop` counts all molecules in a drop (big count); `same-air-less-oxygen` (partial-pressure) keeps the 21% share fixed while pressure changes; `dilute-until-none-left` dilutes one solute to zero. This one is the share scale itself (per cent vs ppm) on a mixture the learner breathes. It makes no greenhouse or climate-effect claim; if a reviewer asks for one, it goes to a separate brief with its own sources.

### 4. acid-diluted-a-thousandfold · concept: water-autoionization · core · demand 2 · format: Single card
- Learner knows: that each pH unit is a factor of 10 in [H+] (ph-log-scale); that dilution lowers concentration (same-sugar-more-water) / does not know: that water makes its own H+ and OH- (about 10^-7 mol/L each at 25 °C), so diluting an acid stalls near pH 7 instead of passing it.
- Misconception: diluting 10 times always raises the pH by 1, so a pH 5 acid diluted 1,000 times becomes pH 8 (basic).
- Prediction (stage text <= 25 words): "Hydrochloric acid at pH 5 is diluted 1,000 times with pure water at 25 °C. Its pH becomes about..." options: A. about 5 / B. about 6 / C. about 7 / D. about 8 (answer: C, pH 6.98).
- Check (node): charge balance [H+] = c + [OH-] with [H+][OH-] = Kw = 1.0e-14, so [H+] = c/2 + sqrt(c^2/4 + Kw). Starting c = 1e-5 mol/L (pH 5.000). Diluted 10x: pH 5.996; 100x: 6.791; 1,000x (c = 1e-8): pH 6.978; 10,000x: 6.998; 100,000x: 7.000. The H+ coming from water ([OH-], since water ionises in pairs) rises from 1.0e-9 to 9.5e-8 mol/L at 1,000x, while the acid's share falls to 1.0e-8: water now supplies about 90% of the H+.
- Locked stage: one vertical log axis of [H+] in mol/L (10^-4 at top to 10^-10, pH labels beside it), a stacked bar split into "from the acid" and "from water itself", pH readout 5.0, dilution readout "x1". No line or label at pH 7 on the locked stage (a pre-drawn limit would point at the answer); the neutral line appears only after the first dilution past x100.
- What the stage shows (explore): control 1 "Dilute x10" (step button) multiplies the dilution and redraws the stacked bar with the formula above; control 2 "Undo" steps back. As the learner taps, the acid segment shrinks a factor of 10 per tap but the total stops falling at 10^-7, because the water segment grows to fill the gap; pH readout to two decimals. Milestones: (1) dilution x1,000 reached (the question's case, pH recorded), (2) one more tap with pH still below 7 (recorded).
- Key state: x1,000 with pH 6.98, the water segment taller than the acid segment.
- Reveal draft (77 words): "At 1,000-fold dilution your acid supplied only 10^-8 mol/L of H+, ten times less than water makes by itself, so the pH stopped at {6.98}, not 8. Water always splits a little: at 25 °C, [H+] × [OH−] stays 10^-14, so neutral water holds 10^-7 mol/L of each. On your bar, the water's share grew as the acid's shrank. Adding water can bring an acid toward pH 7, never past it: only a base makes a solution basic."
- Phone fit: the reveal names the acid share at x1,000, the recorded pH and the two bar segments; the bar (both segments), the pH readout and the dilution readout must be on screen with stage-top crop 0. One axis, one bar, two readouts, two controls.
- Planned claims:
  1. At 25 °C the ion product of water is Kw = [H+][OH−] = about 1.0 x 10^-14 (mol/L)^2, so neutral water has about 10^-7 mol/L of each. support: citation; source: CRC, "Ionization constant of water as a function of temperature and pressure" (pKw about 14.00 at 25 °C); Atkins.
  2. A pH 5 HCl solution diluted 1,000 times with pure water has pH about 6.98, and further dilution approaches 7.00 from below. support: derivation; source: charge balance and Kw (formula above), node arithmetic, the lesson's own computation.
  3. Hydrochloric acid at these concentrations is fully dissociated. support: citation; source: Atkins or any general chemistry text (strong acid).
  4. Assumption: "pure water" here means CO2-free water at 25 °C; water left open to air absorbs CO2 and is slightly acidic. support: stated assumption; source: none needed if no number is given; if a builder quotes a pH for air-saturated water, it needs its own derivation (Henry's law for CO2 plus Ka1 of carbonic acid) and a fact-checker.
  5. Adding pure water to an acid cannot make it basic, because water adds equal amounts of H+ and OH−. support: derivation; source: charge balance above.
- Metadata for meta.json: `also`: ["chemistry:ph-log-scale", "chemistry:dilution", "chemistry:equilibrium-constant"]; references: none.
- curriculum: `{"id":"water-autoionization","name":"Water makes its own ions: dilution stalls at pH 7","level":"core","demand":2,"prerequisites":["ph-log-scale","dilution"]}`
- Needs interaction-designer spec: no.
- Differs from: `ph-log-scale` (coffee against lemon juice) compares two pH values on the log scale; `one-dose-of-acid` adds acid to water and a buffer (its stage solves the same Kw equation, but the idea there is the buffer's stock); `dilute-until-none-left` runs a solute out to zero molecules. This lesson's idea is that the solvent is not inert: water's own ions set a floor that the misconception "x10 = +1 pH forever" ignores. Demand 2, not 3: the misconception is real and common in school chemistry, but narrower than the share-scale and energy-scale briefs above.
