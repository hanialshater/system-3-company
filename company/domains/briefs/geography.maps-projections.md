### 1. sheet-at-half-scale · concept: map-scale-ratio · intro · demand 2
- Format: Single card (preset buttons on one map-sheet picture).
- Wave rank 2/15 (uncovered concept).
- Learner knows: a map says 1:50,000 / does not know: that doubling the number doubles each distance on the ground and so quadruples the land on the same sheet.
- Misconception: Going from 1:50,000 to 1:100,000 puts twice as much land on the sheet.
- Prediction: "A 40 x 40 cm map sheet at 1:50,000 covers 400 km^2 of land. The same size of sheet at 1:100,000 covers:" options: A) 800 km^2 / B) 200 km^2 / C) 1,600 km^2 (answer: C). Check: section `scale`: 40 cm x 50,000 = 20 km, so 400 km^2; at 1:100,000, 40 x 40 km = 1,600 km^2; at 1:25,000, 100 km^2; at 1:250,000, 10,000 km^2.
- Locked stage: a ground grid of 1 km squares (labelled "1 square = 1 km") with the 1:50,000 sheet outlined (20 x 20 squares), readouts hidden.
- Interaction (DOM order): presets "1:100,000" / "1:25,000" / "1:250,000" (milestones `q` for 1:100,000 and `zoom` for 1:25,000; the sheet outline redraws on the ground grid, the 1:50,000 outline stays as a dashed trace; the 1:250,000 sheet zooms the grid out so 100 x 100 squares fit, drawn as 10 km blocks). Readouts: "1 cm on the sheet = X km", "Sheet covers X km x X km = Y km^2". Initial: 1:50,000 (the question's start, not a preset).
- Key state: the 1:100,000 sheet drawn around the dashed 1:50,000 one, visibly four of them.
- Reveal draft (about 70 words): "The sheet covered 1,600 km^2: four times as much, not twice. At 1:100,000 each centimetre stands for 1 km instead of half a kilometre, so the sheet reaches twice as far east and twice as far north. Doubling the scale number doubles distances and quadruples area. That is why a map with the bigger number, a smaller-scale map, shows more land with less detail."
- Recall: "How many times more land fits on the same sheet at 1:250,000 than at 1:50,000?" (25).
- Claims: (1) A scale of 1:N means 1 unit on the map is N units on the ground (definition; Ordnance Survey map-reading guide, or Snyder 1987 introduction). (2) Sheet arithmetic (derivation, section `scale`). (3) "Smaller scale means a bigger N" (definition, same source). (4) The 40 x 40 cm sheet is chosen for round numbers (scope).
- Curriculum metadata: primary `geography:map-scale-ratio`; also [`math:area-scaling`]; prerequisites []; level intro; strand maps-projections.
- Nearest lessons: `britain-on-a-page` (brief only: length conversion), `ruler-on-mercator` (scale varies across one map). This one: area goes with the square of the scale number.

### 2. the-49th-parallel-in-europe · concept: latitude-longitude · intro · demand 3
- Format: Map explorer (Equirectangular WorldMap, Natural Earth 1:110m, North Atlantic window).
- Wave rank 5/15 (demand-3 concept with one lesson, `null-island-lat-lon`).
- Learner knows: lines of latitude circle the globe / does not know: where a familiar parallel lands on the other side of an ocean, and that all of the UK lies north of all of the contiguous United States.
- Misconception: The US-Canada border latitude crosses Europe somewhere through Britain, since both look "northern" on a world map.
- Prediction: "The long straight part of the US-Canada border follows 49 degrees north. Follow that line east across the Atlantic. Where does it reach Europe?" options: A) Scotland / B) England / C) France (answer: C). Check: section `lat49` (1:110m): the contiguous-US outline has 12 vertices on exactly 49.00 N from 122.8 W to 95.2 W; its northernmost point is 49.39 N; the UK's southernmost point is 49.96 N (50.02 N at 1:50m); mainland France spans 42.34 to 51.15 N, so 49 N crosses France and passes south of every UK polygon.
- Locked stage: the map with a latitude line at 40 N, no labels on Europe, no UK or France highlight.
- Interaction (DOM order): presets "45 degrees N" / "49 degrees N" / "52 degrees N" (the line redraws across the whole window; countries the line crosses are outlined; milestone `q` at 49) / "Shade land north of the line" toggle (milestone `shade`: at 49 N the whole UK is shaded and the contiguous US is not). Initial: 40 N. Readout: the line's latitude. Projection named on the stage: "Equirectangular, Natural Earth 1:110m".
- Key state: the 49 N line through France with the UK fully shaded north of it.
- Reveal draft (about 65 words): "The 49th parallel reaches Europe in France, south of every part of the United Kingdom. On this map the UK's southern tip is at 50.0 degrees north, while the contiguous US stops at 49.4. A line along the Canadian border runs below all of Britain. Latitude is one number around the whole globe: the ocean in between does not bend it."
- Recall: "Which is farther north: the southern tip of Great Britain or the northern edge of the contiguous US?" (Great Britain).
- Claims: (1) The straight border segment at 49.00 N between about 123 W and 95 W (data: section `lat49`; the treaty history is not on the card). (2) UK south edge 49.96 N and US north edge 49.39 N at 1:110m (data; state the resolution; the real Lizard Point is about 49.96 N, gazetteer). (3) France spans the parallel (data). (4) Equirectangular maps latitude linearly (Snyder 1987). Hard limit: no claim about why Europe is warm (climate needs data and a source). "Contiguous US" is defined on the card once: "the 48 states south of Canada".
- Curriculum metadata: primary `geography:latitude-longitude`; also [`geography:east-west-surprises`]; prerequisites []; level intro; strand maps-projections.
- Nearest lessons: `null-island-lat-lon` (what the numbers mean), `miami-or-lima-west` (east-west surprise). This one is the north-south counterpart, across an ocean.

### 3. a-circle-on-the-globe · concept: map-projections-area · intro · demand 3
- Format: Map explorer (azimuthal equidistant WorldMap centred on the North Pole, Natural Earth 1:110m).
- Wave rank 6/15 (demand-3 concept whose only lesson, `mercator-greenland`, is still in review).
- Learner knows: flat maps distort / does not know: why no flat map can avoid it: a circle on a sphere is shorter than a flat circle of the same radius, so paper must stretch or tear.
- Misconception: A careful enough projection could flatten the globe without distortion.
- Prediction: "Draw a circle on the Earth: every point 5,000 km from the North Pole, measured along the ground. How long is the circle?" options: A) About 31,000 km / B) About 28,000 km / C) About 40,000 km (answer: B). Check: section `circle`: 2 pi R sin(r/R) = 28,289 km against a flat circle of 31,416 km (10.0% shorter); at 1,000 km 0.4%, at 2,500 km 2.5%, at 10,007.5 km (the equator) 40,030 km against 62,879 km (36.3%).
- Locked stage: the polar map with a circle at 2,500 km and no length readouts.
- Interaction (DOM order): presets "1,000 km" / "5,000 km" / "10,000 km (the equator)" (milestones `q` at 5,000 and `eq`; the circle redraws; a gap wedge shows how much of the drawn circle the true length fills: 36 degrees missing at 5,000 km) / a radius slider. Readouts: "Drawn length on this map" (2 pi r) and "True length on the Earth". Initial: 2,500 km. Stage line: "This map keeps distances from the pole true." (8 words.)
- Key state: 5,000 km, readouts 31,416 and 28,289 km, wedge visible.
- Reveal draft (about 70 words): "About 28,300 km: 10% shorter than the 31,400 km a circle of that radius has on paper. At the equator the gap is 36%. A flat map has to put that circle somewhere: stretch it to the paper's length, as this map does, or cut the paper open. Every flat map of a large area does one or the other, which is why every flat map distorts."
- Recall: "Why must every flat world map distort something?" (circles on a sphere are shorter than flat circles of the same radius).
- Claims: (1) circumference of a circle of surface radius r on a sphere is 2 pi R sin(r/R) (derivation; any spherical-geometry text). (2) R = 6,371.0088 km (IUGG mean radius). (3) The azimuthal equidistant projection keeps distances from its centre true (Snyder 1987 ch. 25; d3-geo `geoAzimuthalEquidistant`). (4) No map of a sphere keeps all distances (Gauss's Theorema Egregium; textbook claim, for example do Carmo 1976; mark `unverified` until read, or state as the derivation in claim 1 applied to every centre).
- Curriculum metadata: primary `geography:map-projections-area`; also [`geography:spherical-triangles`, `geography:equal-area-vs-conformal`]; prerequisites []; level intro; strand maps-projections.
- Nearest lessons: `mercator-greenland` (what distortion does to area), `tissot-circles` (where it goes). This one: why it cannot be avoided.

### 4. cuba-or-madagascar-coastline · concept: coastline-paradox · core · demand 3
- Format: Map explorer (both islands at one shared scale, three data resolutions).
- Wave rank 8/15 (demand-3 concept with one lesson, `coastline-ruler`).
- Learner knows: a coastline's length grows with a shorter ruler (`coastline-ruler`) / does not know: that the map's level of detail is the ruler, and that it can even flip which of two coasts is longer.
- Misconception: Measured on a more detailed map, every coast grows by about the same factor, so comparisons stay fair.
- Prediction: "On Natural Earth's coarse world map (1:110m), Madagascar's outline is 3,869 km and Cuba's 2,800 km. Measured on the same maker's most detailed map (1:10m), which is longer?" options: A) Cuba / B) Madagascar, by even more / C) About the same (answer: A). Check: section `coast`: Cuba 2,800 / 3,625 / 5,746 km at 1:110m / 1:50m / 1:10m (1, 7, 41 polygons); Madagascar 3,869 / 4,288 / 5,455 km (1, 3, 3 polygons). Data catch found in checking: at 1:10m only, Natural Earth draws the US naval base at Guantanamo Bay as a separate feature, so the "Cuba" feature has a 25.3 km land boundary; merged with that feature (topojson `merge`), the island's 1:10m outline is 5,765 km. The lesson measures whole islands: use the merged outline at every resolution (identical to "Cuba" at 1:110m and 1:50m) and label it "the island of Cuba". The answer is unchanged either way (5,746 or 5,765 against 5,455).
- Locked stage: the two 1:110m outlines side by side at the same scale, names and the two coarse lengths only.
- Interaction (DOM order): presets "Medium detail (1:50m)" / "Fine detail (1:10m)" / "Coarse detail (1:110m)" (outlines and lengths redraw; milestones `mid` and `fine`; the coarse button returns, with the fine lengths kept as a trace) / "Zoom on one bay" toggle (a small window of each coast at all three detail levels overlaid; milestone `zoom`). Initial: coarse. Data: the builder extracts the two islands at the three resolutions at build time into one small JSON (about 4,500 vertices at 1:10m; quantise if over 60 KB) and records the script in the claims. No runtime fetch, and the 3.6 MB 1:10m file is never imported by the page.
- Key state: fine detail selected, Cuba's 5,765 km above Madagascar's 5,455 km.
- Reveal draft (about 70 words): "On the fine map Cuba is longer: 5,765 km against Madagascar's 5,455. Cuba's outline doubled with detail; Madagascar's grew by two fifths. Cuba's ragged coast and its dozens of small cays only appear on the fine map, while Madagascar's coast is smoother. A coastline's length belongs to the coast and the map together, so even which of two coasts is longer can depend on the map."
- Recall: "Two atlases rank two countries' coastlines differently. How can both be measuring correctly?" (different detail is a different ruler).
- Claims: (1) the six lengths and polygon counts (data: world-atlas 2.0.2, Natural Earth countries 1:110m, 1:50m, 1:10m; haversine on R; section `coast`). (2) Madagascar has no neighbour at any resolution; Cuba has none at 1:110m and 1:50m and one at 1:10m, the Guantanamo Bay base feature (data: topojson `neighbors`); the merged island outline has no land boundary. The card draws no border inside the island and says nothing about the base (charter: no political claims about borders); the claim records the merge. (3) Length grows with resolution (Richardson effect; Mandelbrot 1967, Science 156; mark `unverified` until read, the data claim stands without it). (4) "Ragged" and "smoother" are judgements about the drawn outlines. Hard limit (charter: no rankings that depend on resolution): the card never states which country "has the longer coastline"; it only says which outline is longer on which map.
- Curriculum metadata: primary `geography:coastline-paradox`; also []; prerequisites [`geography:map-scale-varies`]; level core; strand maps-projections.
- Nearest lesson: `coastline-ruler` (one coast, shorter rulers). This one: real datasets as the ruler and a ranking flip.

### 5. mostly-yes-on-the-map · concept: choropleth-area-bias · intro · demand 2 [toy]
- Format: Before / after toggle (land map, then one tile per 100,000 people).
- Wave rank 12/15 (new concept).
- Learner knows: maps colour regions by a result / does not know: that a filled-region map gives each region ink by its land area, so big thinly settled regions dominate a map about people.
- Misconception: The colour that covers most of the map is what most people answered.
- Prediction: "On this map of a made-up country, the regions that answered Yes cover 78% of the land. What share of the people answered Yes?" options: A) About 50% / B) About 20% / C) About 80% (answer: B). Check: section `choropleth`: 12 regions, 500,000 km^2, 10 million people; the six Yes regions cover 390,000 km^2 (78.0%) and hold 2,000,000 people (20.0%); redrawn as tiles of 100,000 people, Yes is 20 tiles of 100.
- Locked stage: the 12-region map in two neutral fills, legend "Yes / No", no population figures.
- Interaction (DOM order): "Redraw: 1 tile = 100,000 people" (the regions morph into tile blocks sized by people; milestone `people`) / "Show the biggest and the busiest region" (outlines the 90,000 km^2 region with 400,000 people and the 20,000 km^2 one with 2,000,000; milestone `pair`) / "Back to the land map" (milestone `back`; the tile counts stay as a readout). Readouts: share of land Yes, share of people Yes.
- Key state: the tile view, 20 Yes tiles beside 80 No tiles.
- Reveal draft (about 70 words): "Only 20% said Yes. The Yes regions are big and thinly settled: 78% of the land, 2 million of 10 million people. A filled-region map gives each region ink by its area, not its people, so it shows land, not who answered. Redrawn with one tile per 100,000 people, Yes shrinks to 20 tiles of 100. When a map is about people, ask what its colour is counting."
- Recall: "A map is 80% one colour. What do you need to know before saying most people chose that colour?" (how many people live in the coloured regions).
- Claims: (1) all figures from the made-up table (derivation; section `choropleth`; the table is the lesson's data, disclosed). (2) In a choropleth each region's colour fills its whole area, so ink is proportional to land area (derivation). (3) Stage line "Made-up country, made-up question" (scope/judgement). Optional source for the general point: Monmonier 1991, How to Lie with Maps (mark `unverified` until read).
- Cultural rules (people topic; from `company/reviews/schelling-on-new-york/2026-10-14-cultural-review.md`): the country, regions and question are made up and the stage says so; no election, party, vote or real place on the card, in alt text or in code names; the two fills are neutral, differ in lightness and avoid red/blue, red/green, black/white and yellow (R5 of that review); Yes regions also carry a second cue (a small tick mark per region or dot pattern; regions are large, so pattern reads); legend in words, colours never named in text; no word that ranks the people in either kind of region ("rural people", "empty", "backward" are out; use "thinly settled" and "densely settled").
- Curriculum metadata: primary `geography:choropleth-area-bias`; also [`geography:population-distribution`]; prerequisites []; level intro; strand maps-projections.
- Nearest lessons: `half-of-humanity` (where people are), `density-the-typical-person-lives-at` (people-places brief 3). This one: how a map's ink misleads.
