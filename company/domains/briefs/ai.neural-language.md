### 59. long-answers-lose-the-product · concept: sequence-length-bias · core · demand 3
- Format: Single card
- Audience and knowledge boundary: learner knows: a model picks the next token from probabilities (next-word-tally), that a whole text's probability is the product along its path (greedy-word-trap) / does not know: that because every extra token multiplies by a number below 1, the most probable whole answer is biased toward short ones, so search for the single most likely text needs a length correction.
- Misconception: The answer whose every token the model is more confident about is the more probable answer, so "most likely" means "best".
- Prediction (stage text, 22 words): "Answer S has 2 tokens, each 50% likely. Answer L has 10 tokens, each 85% likely. The more probable whole answer is:" options: A) S / B) L / C) they tie (answer: A). The locked stage shows the two token chains with their per-token percentages and empty "whole answer" readouts.
- Check (node, 2026-10-14): S = 0.5^2 = 0.25; L = 0.85^10 = 0.197. L's whole probability for lengths 1-12 at 85% per token: 0.850, 0.722, 0.614, 0.522, 0.444, 0.377, 0.321, 0.272, 0.232, 0.197, 0.167, 0.142; L beats S up to 8 tokens and loses from 9. Per-token average (geometric mean, the length-normalised score): S 50%, L 85%, so L wins at every length.
- What the stage shows: two token chains, S (2 boxes) above L (n boxes), each box showing its probability; a running product fills a bar at the end of each chain as the learner taps "multiply" (one token per tap, both chains in step; S stops at 2). A length slider for L (1 to 12 tokens) re-draws its chain and product. A toggle "score per token" replaces both products with the per-token average, and the winner badge moves. Controls: multiply, L length slider, score per token, Reset (4).
- Key state (milestones): question-case (L at 10, both products complete, 25% and 19.7% recorded), crossover-seen (a length where L wins, 8 or fewer, its product recorded), per-token-on (toggle on at length 10; winner L recorded). Reveal unlocks after question-case and per-token-on.
- Reveal payoff (draft, 77 words): "Every token of L was more likely than any token of S, yet S won: 25% against 19.7%. Each extra token multiplies the whole by something below 1, so ten confident steps lost to two shaky ones. At 8 tokens or fewer, L won. Searching for the single most probable answer therefore favours short ones. Scoring per token, the length-normalised score you switched on, put L ahead. Real decoders add such a length correction for this reason."
- Phone fit (390x844): the reveal points at the two product bars, the winner badge and the per-token toggle state. At 12 tokens, L's chain wraps to two rows of 6 boxes (each >= 44 px wide, percentages >= 10 px rendered); the stage is two chains plus two bars, no table. Stage-top crop 0 px with both chains and bars visible when the reveal opens; the 25% / 19.7% values stay as a faint "product" row while per-token is on.
- Planned claims:
  1. 0.5 x 0.5 = 0.25 and 0.85^10 = 0.197, so the 2-token answer is more probable. support: derivation; source: arithmetic, node.
  2. At 85% per token, a longer answer beats 25% only up to 8 tokens (0.85^8 = 0.272, 0.85^9 = 0.232). support: derivation; source: node.
  3. A sequence's probability is the product of its tokens' conditional probabilities, so each extra token can only lower it. support: derivation (chain rule of probability, each factor <= 1).
  4. Exact or beam search for the most probable output in neural machine translation favours short outputs, and real systems add a length penalty or normalisation. support: citation; source: Wu et al. (2016), "Google's neural machine translation system", arXiv 1609.08144, section 7 (length normalisation); Murray and Chiang (2018), "Correcting length bias in neural machine translation", WMT 2018. About real systems.
  5. (Optional told sentence; cut first) In one NMT study the empty output was the single most probable translation for more than half of the test sentences. support: citation; source: Stahlberg and Byrne (2019), "On NMT search errors and model errors: cat got your tongue?", EMNLP 2019. About real systems; the fact-checker confirms the share before it reaches a learner.
- Curriculum (new concept): `{"id":"sequence-length-bias","name":"Length bias: the most probable answer tends to be short","level":"core","demand":3,"prerequisites":["greedy-decoding"],"subarea":"neural-language"}`; also none; references none; tags decoding, beam search, likelihood.
- Differs from nearest: greedy-word-trap (greedy-decoding) shows greedy missing the most probable sentence, so the full search is the hero; here the full search is the problem, because "most probable" itself prefers short text. keep-the-top-85 (top-p) and temperature-reshapes change one step's distribution, not the scoring of whole answers.

### 73. twice-the-text-pairs · concept: attention-quadratic-cost · core · demand 2
- Format: Single card
- Strand: neural-language
- Audience and knowledge boundary: learner knows: attention scores every word against the others (attention-shares-one), a model reads a limited window (window-forgets-start) / does not know: that because every token is compared with every token, the work grows with the square of the length, so twice the text costs four times the comparisons, which is one reason context windows are limited.
- Misconception: Twice as much text costs a model twice as much work to read.
- Prediction (stage text, 22 words): "Attention compares every token with every token. A 1,000-token text needs 1 million comparisons. A 2,000-token text needs:" options: A) 2 million / B) 4 million / C) 8 million (answer: B). The locked stage shows an empty square grid frame and a token row, no cells.
- Check (node, 2026-10-14): n x n comparisons: 4 -> 16, 8 -> 64, 16 -> 256, 1,000 -> 1,000,000, 2,000 -> 4,000,000. With a causal mask (each token looks only back), n(n + 1)/2: 10, 36, 500,500, 2,001,000, still about 4x when n doubles.
- What the stage shows: a token row along the top and left of a square grid; each cell is one comparison. Preset buttons in DOM order: "4 tokens", "8 tokens", "1,000 tokens", "2,000 tokens", Reset (5). At 4 and 8 tokens every cell is drawn (16, 64); at 1,000 and 2,000 the grid is a shaded square whose area is to scale (the 2,000 square has twice the side of the 1,000 square, drawn over it as an outline). Readout: "2,000 tokens · 4,000,000 comparisons".
- Key state (milestones): eight-seen (64 recorded, with 16 from the 4-token press if made), thousand-seen (1,000,000), big-case (4,000,000 recorded). Reveal unlocks after eight-seen and big-case.
- Reveal payoff (draft, 72 words): "Doubling from 4 to 8 tokens took the grid from 16 cells to 64, and from 1,000 tokens to 2,000 took it from 1 million comparisons to 4 million. Each new token is compared with all the others, and they all with it, so the grid grows in both directions. Twice the text, four times the work. That is one reason long contexts are expensive and why windows have limits."
- Phone fit (390x844): the reveal points at the 8-token grid (kept as a small inset, 64 cells of 12 px) and the two big squares. Main square 220 px side (the 1,000 outline 110 px), inset 100 px; token labels on the 8-grid at 10 px; readout one line. Stage-top crop 0 px with squares, inset and readout visible when the reveal opens.
- Planned claims:
  1. Comparing every token with every token takes n^2 comparisons: 1,000,000 for 1,000 tokens and 4,000,000 for 2,000. support: derivation.
  2. With a causal mask the count is n(n + 1)/2, which also roughly quadruples when n doubles. support: derivation; source: node.
  3. Self-attention's cost per layer grows with the square of the sequence length (O(n^2 d)). support: citation; source: Vaswani et al. (2017), "Attention is all you need", NeurIPS 2017, Table 1. About real systems.
- Curriculum (new concept): `{"id":"attention-quadratic-cost","name":"Attention cost grows with the square of the text","level":"core","demand":2,"prerequisites":["attention-weights"],"subarea":"neural-language"}`; also cs:big-o-growth; references none; tags transformers, attention, context.
- Differs from nearest: attention-shares-one (attention-weights) is about how one token's weights sum to one; this is about how many weights there are. window-forgets-start (context-window) shows what a window drops; this shows why windows cost. The cs big-o-growth lesson compares growth rates in general; this one ties n^2 to a concrete model.

### 74. one-filter-every-place · concept: convolution-weight-sharing · core · demand 2
- Format: Single card
- Strand: neural-language
- Audience and knowledge boundary: learner knows: a neuron is a weighted sum of inputs (one-nudge-per-mistake) / does not know: that a convolution slides one small set of weights over every position, so a pattern learned in one place is found in every place, with far fewer weights than one detector per position.
- Misconception: A network that learned to spot a pattern in one part of an image has to learn it again for every other part.
- Prediction (stage text, 22 words): "A sliding filter learned this 3-pixel pattern only from strips with it at the left. Now it appears at the right. The response:" options: A) zero / B) the same as at the left / C) half as strong (answer: B). The locked stage shows a 12-pixel strip with the pattern at the right, the 3-weight filter beside it, and an empty response row.
- Check (node, 2026-10-14): pattern pixels 1, 0, 1; filter weights 1, -1, 1; strip of 12 pixels, zeros elsewhere; 10 window positions. Shared filter, pattern at left (pixels 0-2): responses 2, -1, 1, 0, ..., max 2; at right (pixels 9-11): 0, ..., 1, -1, 2, max 2. One detector per position, trained only where the pattern appeared (windows 0-2 get weights 1, -1, 1; windows 3-9 stay at 0): left max 2, right max 0. Weights: shared 3; one per position 10 x 3 = 30.
- What the stage shows: the 12-pixel strip, the filter window drawn at each position in turn as a faint outline, and a 10-cell response row under it (each cell its response value). Preset buttons in DOM order: "Slide the shared filter", "Use one detector per place", "Move pattern to the left", Reset (4). "Use one detector per place" re-scores the current strip with the per-position detectors (cells 3-9 marked "untrained"). Readout: "shared filter · 3 weights · best response 2".
- Key state (milestones): right-shared (shared filter, pattern at right; max 2 recorded), right-separate (per-place detectors; max 0, 30 weights recorded), left-seen (pattern at left; max 2 for both). Reveal unlocks after right-shared and right-separate.
- Reveal payoff (draft, 74 words): "The shared filter had only ever seen the pattern at the left, yet at the right it responded 2, as strongly as at the left. It is one set of 3 weights slid over every position, so whatever it learns in one place it finds everywhere. The detectors with their own weights for each place responded 0 at the right: those 30 weights never learned. Sharing weights is how image networks reuse what they learn."
- Phone fit (390x844): the reveal points at the strip, the response row and the readout. 12 pixels at 28 px (336 px), response row of 10 cells at 32 px with values at 12 px; about 150 px. Stage-top crop 0 px with strip, row and readout visible when the reveal opens; the shared-filter row stays as a faint second row after "one detector per place".
- Planned claims:
  1. Sliding the filter 1, -1, 1 over the strip gives a peak response of 2 wherever the pattern 1, 0, 1 sits. support: derivation; source: node.
  2. In the toy, per-position detectors trained only at the left respond 0 to the pattern at the right; they hold 30 weights against the shared filter's 3. support: derivation (the toy's stated training).
  3. Convolutional networks share the same weights across positions, which cuts the number of parameters and makes a feature detected in one place detectable in others (translation equivariance). support: citation; source: Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville, Deep Learning (MIT Press, 2016), section 9.2; LeCun, Bottou, Bengio and Haffner (1998), "Gradient-based learning applied to document recognition", Proceedings of the IEEE 86(11): 2278-2324. About real systems.
- Curriculum (new concept): `{"id":"convolution-weight-sharing","name":"Convolution: one filter slid over every place","level":"core","demand":2,"prerequisites":["perceptron-learning"],"subarea":"neural-language"}`; also none; references none (the work is not in content/works.json; it is cited in the claims); tags convolution, vision, neural networks.
- Differs from nearest: background-shortcut (shortcut-learning) is about which cue an image model learns; this is about where it can use it. one-nudge-per-mistake (perceptron-learning) has one weight per input; here the weights are reused across positions.
