### 1. towel-spread-dries-first · concept: surface-area-drying · intro · demand 3
Safety: pass (company/reviews/towel-spread-dries-first/2026-10-13-safety.md)
- Learner knows: wet things dry in air; a breeze helps. / does not know: that water leaves only from a surface that touches air, so drying time follows exposed area, not the amount of cloth.
- Misconception: It is the same towel and the same water, so folded or spread it dries in about the same time.
- Prediction: "Two identical wet bath towels hang in the same room. One is spread flat; it dries in about 3 hours. The other hangs folded in quarters. How long does it take?" options: About 3 hours: same water, same air / About 6 hours / About 12 hours (answer: C). Check (node): a 0.7 x 1.4 m towel spread has two faces, 1.96 m^2 of exposed area. Folded in quarters the stack shows 2 x 0.245 = 0.49 m^2 plus about 0.03 m^2 of edges, so about 3.8 times less area. In the constant-rate drying model the time scales with water mass / exposed area: 3 h x 3.8 ≈ 11.4 h, "about 12 hours". The real folded towel is slower still, because the inner layers must wick water to the outside.
- Interaction: a fold control (spread / half / quarters) and an air toggle (still / breeze). The stage shows the towel edge-on, with arrows leaving only from faces that touch air, an exposed-area readout and a drying-time readout. One representation: the edge-on towel.
- Reveal: Water escapes only from surfaces that touch air. Folding hides three quarters of the surface, so the same water leaves through a quarter of the doorway. A breeze speeds both, but it cannot reach the inner layers. Spread it out, then add air.
- Claims a learner will see, with planned sources: (1) Evaporation happens at the surface exposed to air; in the constant-rate drying period the rate is proportional to that area (mass transfer: Incropera, DeWitt et al., *Fundamentals of Heat and Mass Transfer*, evaporation chapter; or Mujumdar, *Handbook of Industrial Drying*, constant-rate period). (2) A 0.7 x 1.4 m towel spread flat exposes 1.96 m^2; folded in quarters about 0.52 m^2 (arithmetic; towel size illustrative, stated). (3) "About 12 hours" comes from the stated model, not a measurement; real times depend on humidity, temperature and airflow (stated on the stage). (4) Moving air removes the humid layer next to the cloth and speeds evaporation (same mass-transfer source: the mass-transfer coefficient rises with air speed).
- Nearest lessons: chemistry/the-fast-ones-leave (why evaporation cools) and chemistry/cut-the-cube-surface (surface area and reaction rate). This one is the household decision: same water, different area.
- Format: Before / after toggle (folded → spread; the spread state persists into the reveal).
- Safety class: none (laundry, no heat source; the stage must not suggest drying on a heater, which would need a warning).
- Illustrations: hero 1:1 (request `towel-spread-dries-first`); before/after pair 4:3 (request `towel-spread-dries-first-pair`).

### 2. spaced-beats-cram · concept: spaced-repetition-study · intro · demand 3
Safety: pass (company/reviews/spaced-beats-cram/2026-10-13-safety.md)
- Learner knows: you forget things over time; reviewing helps. / does not know: the spacing effect, which says that the same study time split across days is remembered much longer than one block.
- Misconception: Cramming works: it got me through tomorrow's test, so it must be the efficient way to learn.
- Prediction: "Same 3 hours of study. Plan A: all 3 hours the night before. Plan B: one hour on three different days. A month later, which plan remembers more?" options: Plan A / Plan B / About the same: same 3 hours (answer: B). Check: across hundreds of experiments, spaced practice beats massed practice at long retention intervals for the same total time (Cepeda et al. 2006). The twist the stage shows: on the morning after, Plan A can look as good or better.
- Interaction: a time scrubber (day 0 to day 30) and a plan selector (A / B / both). Two memory curves drawn from a stated illustrative forgetting model: each review lifts memory and makes the next decline slower. Test-day markers at "next morning" and "one month". The learner must visit both markers to earn the reveal.
- Reveal: On the morning after, the cram looks fine. A month later it has mostly faded, while the spaced plan holds. Each gap lets you forget a little, and retrieving it again is what makes it stick. Same hours, different calendar.
- Claims a learner will see, with planned sources: (1) For the same total study time, spaced (distributed) study gives better long-term retention than massed study (Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer 2006, *Psychological Bulletin* 132(3), 354–380, a meta-analysis of hundreds of experiments). (2) Massed study can match or beat spaced study on a test given right after study (Rawson & Kintsch 2005, *Journal of Educational Psychology* 97(1), 70–80, rereading and time of test; and Cepeda et al. 2006). (3) Distributed practice is rated a "high utility" learning technique (Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan & Willingham 2013, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 14(1), 4–58). (4) The curves are an illustrative model, not measured data; the percentages on the stage are not predictions for any learner (stated on the stage). (5) Longer gaps help more when the test is further away (Cepeda et al. 2008, *Psychological Science* 19(11), 1095–1102) only if the builder shows a gap control; otherwise leave it out.
- Nearest lessons: none in any category on human memory (ai/window-forgets-start is about model context, not people).
- Format: Storyboard / scrub (a time slider with key frames at the two test days).
- Safety class: none (study habits; no health or sleep claims. Do not mention all-nighters or sleep loss; that is a health claim for a later brief).
- Illustrations: hero 1:1 (request `spaced-beats-cram`).

### 3. wobbly-table-quarter-turn · concept: wobbly-table-turn · core · demand 2
Safety: pass with changes (company/reviews/wobbly-table-quarter-turn/2026-10-13-safety.md)
- Learner knows: a four-legged table rocks on an uneven floor; people fold a napkin under a leg. / does not know: that rotating a table with four equal legs on a continuous floor always finds a position where all four feet touch, within a quarter turn (an intermediate value argument).
- Misconception: A rocking table needs a wedge or a new spot; turning it in place just moves the wobble around.
- Prediction: "A square café table with four equal legs rocks on bumpy paving. Without a wedge or moving it, what fixes it?" options: Nothing: it needs a wedge / Turn it in place, less than a quarter turn / Only a full half turn can help (answer: B). Check: put three feet on the ground and measure the fourth foot's height above (+) or below (−) the ground. A 90° turn swaps the roles of the diagonals, so the sign flips; with a continuous floor the gap passes through zero somewhere between 0° and 90°.
- Interaction: a rotation slider (0° to 90°) on a top-down view of the table over a bumpy floor, with a side strip showing the fourth leg's gap and a signed gap readout; a floor selector (two bump patterns) so the zero is not always at the same angle; a "legs" toggle (equal / one short) that shows the case where turning cannot help. Milestone: the gap reaches zero.
- Reveal: At the start one diagonal pair rocks. A quarter turn later the other pair would rock, so somewhere in between the gap had to cross zero: all four feet down. It needs equal legs and a floor without steps or holes; if one leg is short, turning will not save you.
- Claims a learner will see, with planned sources: (1) For a table with four equal legs whose feet lie on a circle (a square table is one), on continuous ground that is not too steep, a rotation of at most 90° puts all four feet on the ground (Baritompa, Löwen, Polster & Ross 2007, "Mathematical table turning revisited", *The Mathematical Intelligencer* 29(2), 49–58; Martin 2006, "On the stability of four feet tables", arXiv preprint; fact-checker to find the id). (2) The sign of the fourth leg's gap flips after a quarter turn, so it must pass through zero (intermediate value theorem; any calculus text). (3) Balanced means all four feet touch, not that the top is level (stated). (4) The steepness limit (about 35° for a square table in Baritompa et al. 2007) is far steeper than any café floor (fact-checker to confirm the figure). (5) The bump patterns are illustrative (stated).
- Nearest lessons: none on the intermediate value theorem; math lessons on geometry do not cover it.
- Format: Single card.
- Safety class: none (no heat, no tools; the image must not show anyone standing on the table).
- Illustrations: hero 1:1 (request `wobbly-table-quarter-turn`).

### 4. square-bow-stays-tied · concept: shoelace-square-bow · intro · demand 2
Safety: pass (company/reviews/square-bow-stays-tied/2026-10-13-safety.md)
- Learner knows: how to tie a bow; bows come undone when you walk or run. / does not know: that a shoelace bow is either a square (reef) knot or a granny knot depending on the direction of the second wrap, that the two look alike, and that the granny version fails much sooner.
- Misconception: Laces come undone because the bow was not pulled tight enough, or the laces are slippery; the fix is a double knot.
- Prediction: "Two bows, same laces, pulled equally tight. Bow A sits across the shoe. Bow B twists so its loops point toe to heel. After a run, which is more likely still tied?" options: Bow A / Bow B / No difference: same laces, same tension (answer: A). Check: a bow that sits along the shoe is the tell-tale sign of the granny-knot version; the square-knot version sits across (Fieggen's shoelace guide; Daily-Diamond et al. 2017 tested both and found the square-based bow the stronger).
- Interaction: two shoes side by side sharing one "walk 10 steps" button; for each shoe a toggle for the direction of the loop wrap (over / under). Flipping the wrap turns a granny bow into a square bow, and the drawn bow rotates from along to across. The walk button loosens each knot by a stated illustrative amount per step, faster for the granny. Milestone: the learner flips at least one wrap and walks.
- Reveal: Both bows started equally tight. The granny version twists along the shoe and works loose: each footstrike loosens it and the swinging ends pull it apart. Reverse the direction of the loop wrap, and the same bow sits across the shoe and holds. Check the direction before reaching for a double knot.
- Claims a learner will see, with planned sources: (1) A standard shoelace bow is a slipped reef (square) knot; tying the second step in the same direction as the first gives a slipped granny knot instead (Ashley, *The Ashley Book of Knots*, 1944, reef and granny knots; Fieggen, "Ian's Shoelace Site", granny knot pages). (2) A granny bow tends to sit along the shoe; a square bow sits across it (Fieggen; the fact-checker checks this against Ashley or a second source). (3) In treadmill and pendulum tests, knot failure came from the footstrike loosening the knot together with the whipping of the free ends and loops; once loosening began, failure followed within a few strides (Daily-Diamond, Gregg & O'Reilly 2017, *Proceedings of the Royal Society A* 473: 20160770). (4) In the same study the square-based bow outlasted the granny-based bow (same source; fact-checker to confirm the exact wording, and drop the claim if the paper does not support it). (5) The loosening rates on the stage are illustrative, not measured (stated).
- Nearest lessons: none on knots.
- Format: Side-by-side comparison (two shoes, one shared walk control, the difference highlighted).
- Safety class: none.
- Illustrations: hero 1:1 (request `square-bow-stays-tied`); comparison pair 4:3, square bow vs granny bow (request `square-bow-stays-tied-pair`). The knot geometry is accuracy-critical.

### 5. long-cord-knots-itself · concept: cord-tangle-length · intro · demand 2
Safety: pass (company/reviews/long-cord-knots-itself/2026-10-13-safety.md)
- Learner knows: cords and cables tangle in bags and drawers. / does not know: that the chance of a real knot rises steeply with length and then levels off, and that a knot needs a free end to pass through a loop.
- Misconception: Tangling is bad luck; a longer cord is about as likely to knot as a short one, or always knots.
- Prediction: "A string is tumbled in a box for 10 seconds, then taken out. Shorter than half a metre, it almost never comes out knotted. What about a 3 m string?" options: Still rarely, about 1 time in 20 / About half the time / Nearly every time (answer: B). Check: in Raymer & Smith's 3,415 tumbling trials, knots almost never formed below about 0.46 m, the probability rose steeply with length, and for long strings it levelled off at about 50% (to be confirmed against the paper's figure by the fact-checker).
- Interaction: a length slider (0.3 m to 4 m), "tumble once" and "tumble 20 times" buttons with a live tally of knotted / not knotted, and an "ends clipped together" toggle. Runs are seeded (mulberry32, seed disclosed) and each trial draws from the published knot probability for that length; the stage does not simulate the physics. Milestone: the learner runs 20 tumbles at two different lengths.
- Reveal: Your tally says it: short strings almost never knotted; long ones knotted about half the time. A knot forms when a free end weaves through a coil, and a longer string has more coils to weave through. Clip the two ends together and a true knot cannot form, because a closed loop cannot tie itself.
- Claims a learner will see, with planned sources: (1) In a tumbled box, strings shorter than about 0.46 m almost never knotted; knotting probability rose sharply with length and levelled off near 50% for long strings (Raymer & Smith 2007, "Spontaneous knotting of an agitated string", *PNAS* 104(42), 16432–16437). (2) Stiffer strings knotted less, and longer tumbling knotted more (same source; include only if the stage shows it). (3) A knot forms when a free end passes through a loop (same source, the braid-move model). (4) A closed loop cannot change its knot type without passing through itself, so a cord with its ends joined cannot form a new true knot; it can still bunch up (knot theory definition: Adams, *The Knot Book*, ch. 1). (5) Each tumble on the stage is a seeded random draw from the published probability curve, read off the paper's figure, approximately (disclosed, pattern `disclose-chosen-seeds`).
- Nearest lessons: probability lessons on simulation runs share the format, not the idea; nothing on knots.
- Format: Simulation run (seeded, tallies; the learner's own run is quoted in the reveal).
- Safety class: none (the hero shows a plain cord or string, not a powered cable).
- Illustrations: hero 1:1 (request `long-cord-knots-itself`).
