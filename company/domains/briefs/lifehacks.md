### 1. towel-spread-dries-first · concept: surface-area-drying · intro · demand 3
Safety: pass (company/reviews/towel-spread-dries-first/2026-10-13-safety.md)
- Learner knows: wet things dry in air; a breeze helps. / does not know: that water leaves only from a surface that touches air, so drying time follows exposed area, not the amount of cloth.
- Misconception: It is the same towel and the same water, so folded or spread it dries in about the same time.
- Prediction: "Two identical wet bath towels hang in the same room. One is spread flat; it dries in about 3 hours. The other hangs folded in quarters. How long does it take?" options: About 3 hours: same water, same air / About 6 hours / About 12 hours (answer: C). Check (node): a 0.7 x 1.4 m towel spread has two faces, 1.96 m^2 of exposed area. Folded in quarters the stack shows 2 x 0.245 = 0.49 m^2 plus about 0.03 m^2 of edges (assuming a stack about 1.5 cm thick, illustrative), 0.52 m^2 in all, so about 3.77 times less area. In the constant-rate drying model the time scales with water mass / exposed area: 3 h x 3.77 ≈ 11.3 h, "about 12 hours". The real folded towel is slower still, because the inner layers must wick water to the outside (the falling-rate period, which the model leaves out).
- Interaction: a fold control (spread / half / quarters) and an air toggle (still / breeze). The stage shows the towel edge-on, with arrows leaving only from faces that touch air, an exposed-area readout and a drying-time readout. One representation: the edge-on towel.
- Reveal: Water escapes only from surfaces that touch air. Folding hides three quarters of the surface, so the same water leaves through a quarter of the doorway. A breeze speeds both, but it cannot reach the inner layers. Spread it out, then add air.
- Claims a learner will see, with planned sources: (1) Evaporation happens at the surface exposed to air; in the constant-rate drying period the rate is proportional to that area (Mujumdar (ed.), *Handbook of Industrial Drying*, 4th ed., CRC Press 2014, ch. 1 "Principles, classification, and selection of dryers", section on the constant-rate period; or Incropera, DeWitt, Bergman & Lavine, *Fundamentals of Heat and Mass Transfer*, 7th ed., Wiley 2011, §6.7 "The heat and mass transfer analogy" / §6.7.2 "Evaporative cooling". Section numbers from memory: the fact-checker confirms the page before verification). (2) A 0.7 x 1.4 m towel spread flat exposes 1.96 m^2; folded in quarters about 0.52 m^2, including about 0.03 m^2 of edges for a stack about 1.5 cm thick (arithmetic; towel size and stack thickness illustrative, stated). (3) "About 12 hours" comes from the stated model, not a measurement; real times depend on humidity, temperature and airflow (stated on the stage). (4) Moving air removes the humid layer next to the cloth and speeds evaporation (Incropera et al., 7th ed., §7.2, flat-plate external flow: the Sherwood number, and so the mass-transfer coefficient, rises with the Reynolds number; page to confirm).
- Nearest lessons: chemistry/the-fast-ones-leave (why evaporation cools) and chemistry/cut-the-cube-surface (surface area and reaction rate). This one is the household decision: same water, different area.
- Format: Before / after toggle (folded → spread; the spread state persists into the reveal).
- Safety class: none (confirmed by the safety review; laundry, no heat source; the stage must not suggest drying on a heater, which would need a warning).
- Illustrations: hero 1:1 (request `towel-spread-dries-first`); before/after pair 4:3 (request `towel-spread-dries-first-pair`).

### 2. spaced-beats-cram · concept: spaced-repetition-study · intro · demand 3
Safety: pass (company/reviews/spaced-beats-cram/2026-10-13-safety.md)
- Learner knows: you forget things over time; reviewing helps. / does not know: the spacing effect, which says that the same study time split across days is remembered much longer than one block.
- Misconception: Cramming works: it got me through tomorrow's test, so it must be the efficient way to learn.
- Prediction: "Same 3 hours of study. Plan A: all 3 hours the night before. Plan B: one hour on three different days. A month later, which plan remembers more?" options: Plan A / Plan B / About the same: same 3 hours (answer: B). Check: across 317 experiments on verbal recall, spaced practice beats massed practice at long retention intervals for the same total time (Cepeda et al. 2006). The twist the stage shows: on the morning after, Plan A can look as good or better (Rawson & Kintsch 2005).
- Interaction: a time scrubber (day 0 to day 30) and a plan selector (A / B / both). Two memory curves drawn from a stated illustrative forgetting model: each review lifts memory and makes the next decline slower. Test-day markers at "next morning" and "one month". The learner must visit both markers to earn the reveal. Each Plan B session is framed as starting by recalling what was learned last time (so the reveal's retrieval line matches what the stage shows); it must not be shown as one uninterrupted reread.
- Reveal: On the morning after, the cram looks fine. A month later it has mostly faded, while the spaced plan holds. Each gap lets you forget a little, and retrieving it again is what makes it stick. Same hours, different calendar.
- Claims a learner will see, with planned sources: (1) For the same total study time, spaced (distributed) study gives better long-term retention than massed study, in verbal recall tasks (Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer 2006, "Distributed practice in verbal recall tasks: A review and quantitative synthesis", *Psychological Bulletin* 132(3), 354–380: 839 assessments in 317 experiments). (2) Massed study can match or beat spaced study on a test given right after study (Rawson & Kintsch 2005, "Rereading effects depend on time of test", *Journal of Educational Psychology* 97(1), 70–80: massed rereading did best on an immediate test, distributed rereading on a test two days later). Cepeda 2006 is not a source for this claim. (3) Distributed practice is rated a "high utility" learning technique (Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan & Willingham 2013, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 14(1), 4–58, DOI 10.1177/1529100612453266). (4) The curves are an illustrative model, not measured data; the percentages on the stage are not predictions for any learner (stated on the stage). (5) Longer gaps help more when the test is further away (Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted & Pashler 2008, "Spacing effects in learning: A temporal ridgeline of optimal retention", *Psychological Science* 19(11), 1095–1102) only if the builder shows a gap control; otherwise leave it out. (6) The reveal line "retrieving it again is what makes it stick": recalling material (practice testing) improves long-term retention more than restudying it for the same time (Roediger & Karpicke 2006, "Test-enhanced learning: Taking memory tests improves long-term retention", *Psychological Science* 17(3), 249–255; and Dunlosky et al. 2013, which rates practice testing "high utility" alongside distributed practice). Cited from memory: the fact-checker confirms the record. If it fails, the reveal line is rewritten to "coming back to it after a gap is what makes it stick", which claim 1 covers.
- Nearest lessons: none in any category on human memory (ai/window-forgets-start is about model context, not people).
- Format: Storyboard / scrub (a time slider with key frames at the two test days).
- Safety class: none (confirmed by the safety review; study habits; no health or sleep claims. Do not mention all-nighters or sleep loss; that is a health claim for a later brief. No "boost your memory" or "brain health" framing).
- Illustrations: hero 1:1 (request `spaced-beats-cram`).

### 3. wobbly-table-quarter-turn · concept: wobbly-table-turn · core · demand 2
Safety: pass with changes (company/reviews/wobbly-table-quarter-turn/2026-10-13-safety.md); the three required changes are applied below and in the hero request, awaiting the reviewer's re-check
- Learner knows: a four-legged table rocks on an uneven floor; people fold a napkin under a leg. / does not know: that rotating a square table with four equal legs on a continuous floor always finds a position where all four feet touch, within a quarter turn (an intermediate value argument).
- Misconception: A rocking table needs a wedge or a new spot; turning it in place just moves the wobble around.
- Prediction: "A square café table with four equal legs rocks on gently uneven paving (no steps or lips between the stones). Without a wedge or moving it, what fixes it?" options: Nothing: it needs a wedge / Turn it in place, less than a quarter turn / Only a full half turn can help (answer: B). Check: put three feet on the ground and measure the fourth foot's height above (+) or below (−) the ground. A 90° turn swaps the roles of the diagonals, so the sign flips; with a continuous floor the gap passes through zero somewhere between 0° and 90°.
- Interaction: a rotation slider (0° to 90°) on a top-down view of the table over a bumpy floor, with a side strip showing the fourth leg's gap and a signed gap readout; a floor selector (two smooth bump patterns, no steps) so the zero is not always at the same angle; a "legs" toggle (equal / one short) that shows the case where turning cannot help. Milestone: the gap reaches zero. The stage shows an empty table top.
- Reveal: At the start one diagonal pair rocks. A quarter turn later the other pair would rock, so somewhere in between the gap had to cross zero: all four feet down. It needs equal legs and a floor without steps or holes; if one leg is short, turning will not save you. Four feet down is not level: on a real slope, things slide. Clear hot drinks before you turn it.
- Claims a learner will see, with planned sources: (1) For a square table with four equal legs, on continuous ground that is not too steep, a rotation of less than 90° puts all four feet on the ground (Martin 2007, "On the stability of four-legged tables", *Physics Letters A* 360(4–5), 495–500, DOI 10.1016/j.physleta.2006.08.053, arXiv math-ph/0510065; Baritompa, Löwen, Polster & Ross 2007, "Mathematical table turning revisited", *The Mathematical Intelligencer* 29(2), 49–58, arXiv math/0511490). Square tables only: the case of feet on a circle is a conjecture, and the brief does not claim it. (2) The sign of the fourth leg's gap flips after a quarter turn, so it must pass through zero (intermediate value theorem: Stewart, *Calculus: Early Transcendentals*, 8th ed., Cengage 2015, §2.5 "Continuity", the Intermediate Value Theorem; section from memory, the fact-checker confirms; the derivation is also shown on the stage and checked as a derivation). (3) Balanced means all four feet touch, not that the top is level: "Four feet down is not level: on a real slope, things slide" (stated on the stage; Baritompa et al. 2007 and Martin 2007). (4) The theorem's mathematical limit: it needs the ground's slope between any two points to stay below arctan(1/√2), about 35°, and legs at least half the table's diagonal long (Baritompa et al. 2007). This is stated only as a condition of the theorem, never as advice about where a table can stand; the card says nothing that suggests using a table on a steep slope. (5) The bump patterns are illustrative (stated). (6) "Clear hot drinks before you turn it" (safety line required by the safety review; advice, not a factual claim; recorded in meta.json as such).
- Nearest lessons: none on the intermediate value theorem; math lessons on geometry do not cover it.
- Format: Single card.
- Safety class: none (kept by the safety review once its changes are made; no heat, no tools; the image must not show anyone standing on the table, nor any cup, glass, plate or anything hot on it).
- Illustrations: hero 1:1 (request `wobbly-table-quarter-turn`).

### 4. square-bow-stays-tied · concept: shoelace-square-bow · intro · demand 2
Safety: pass (company/reviews/square-bow-stays-tied/2026-10-13-safety.md)
- Learner knows: how to tie a bow; bows come undone when you walk or run. / does not know: that a shoelace bow is either a square (reef) knot or a granny knot depending on the direction of the loop wrap, that the two look alike, and that the granny version fails much sooner.
- Misconception: Laces come undone because the bow was not pulled tight enough, or the laces are slippery; the fix is a double knot. (This line is for the designer; it must not reach the learner as "double knots are wrong".)
- Prediction: "Two bows, same laces, pulled equally tight. Bow A sits across the shoe. Bow B twists so its loops point toe to heel. After a run, which is more likely still tied?" options: Bow A / Bow B / No difference: same laces, same tension (answer: A). Check: a bow that sits along the shoe is the tell-tale sign of the granny-knot version; the square-knot version sits across (Fieggen's shoelace guide). Daily-Diamond et al. 2017 tested both: under the heaviest load the granny-based bow failed in every trial, the square-based bow in about half.
- Interaction: two shoes side by side sharing one "walk 10 steps" button; for each shoe a toggle for the direction of the loop wrap (over / under), drawn from Fieggen's diagrams, not from the phrase "same direction". Flipping the wrap turns a granny bow into a square bow, and the drawn bow rotates from along to across. The walk button loosens each knot by a stated illustrative amount per step, faster for the granny; the square bow loosens more slowly, never "never". Milestone: the learner flips at least one wrap and walks.
- Reveal: Both bows started equally tight. The granny version twists along the shoe and works loose: each footstrike loosens it and the swinging ends pull it apart. Reverse the direction of the loop wrap, and the same bow sits across the shoe and holds longer. Check the direction before reaching for a double knot.
- Claims a learner will see, with planned sources: (1) A standard shoelace bow is a slipped reef (square) knot; reversing the loop-wrap step gives a slipped granny knot instead (Fieggen, "Ian's Shoelace Site", Granny Knot page, fieggen.com/shoelace/grannyknot.htm; Ashley, *The Ashley Book of Knots*, Doubleday 1944, reef knot and granny knot entries: the builder gives the ABOK entry numbers, believed to be #1402 for the reef knot and #1214 for the shoelace bow, and the fact-checker confirms them). (2) A granny bow tends to sit along the shoe; a square bow sits across it (Fieggen, primary source; Ashley does not state this, so it is not cited for this claim). (3) In treadmill and pendulum tests, knot failure came from the footstrike loosening the knot together with the whipping of the free ends and loops; once loosening began, failure followed within a few strides (Daily-Diamond, Gregg & O'Reilly 2017, "The roles of impact and inertia in the failure of a shoelace knot", *Proceedings of the Royal Society A* 473: 20160770). (4) In the same study's pendulum tests at the heaviest added weight, the granny-based ("weak") bow failed in every trial, while the square-based ("strong") bow came apart in about half of them (same source; exact wording to be confirmed in the full text). No multiple ("twice as long") appears on the stage: that figure is a co-author's interview estimate, not a result in the paper. (5) The loosening rates on the stage are illustrative, not measured (stated).
- Nearest lessons: none on knots.
- Format: Side-by-side comparison (two shoes, one shared walk control, the difference highlighted).
- Safety class: none (confirmed by the safety review).
- Illustrations: hero 1:1 (request `square-bow-stays-tied`); comparison pair 4:3, square bow vs granny bow (request `square-bow-stays-tied-pair`). The knot geometry is accuracy-critical.

### 5. long-cord-knots-itself · concept: cord-tangle-length · intro · demand 2
Safety: pass (company/reviews/long-cord-knots-itself/2026-10-13-safety.md)
- Learner knows: cords and cables tangle in bags and drawers. / does not know: that the chance of a real knot rises steeply with length and then levels off, and that a knot needs a free end to pass through a loop.
- Misconception: Tangling is bad luck; a longer cord is about as likely to knot as a short one, or always knots.
- Prediction: "A string is tumbled for 10 seconds in a 30 cm box turning once a second, then taken out. Shorter than half a metre, it almost never comes out knotted. What about a 3 m string?" options: Still rarely, about 1 time in 20 / About half the time / Nearly every time (answer: B). Check: in Raymer & Smith's 3,415 tumbling trials, in their standard setup (a 0.30 m cubic box rotated at 1 revolution per second for 10 s, one string type), knots almost never formed below about 0.46 m, the probability rose steeply from 0.46 to 1.5 m, and from 1.5 to 6 m it levelled off at about 50%. Other setups changed it: 3 m strings knotted 26% to 55% of the time depending on box size, 74% after 30 s, and 65% with a more flexible string.
- Interaction: a length slider (0.3 m to 4 m), "tumble once" and "tumble 20 times" buttons with a live tally of knotted / not knotted, and an "ends clipped together" toggle. The setup (30 cm box, 1 turn per second, 10 s) is printed on the stage. Runs are seeded (mulberry32, seed disclosed) and each trial draws from the published knot probability for that length in the standard setup; the stage does not simulate the physics. No speed control (faster rotation reduced knotting in the paper). Milestone: the learner runs 20 tumbles at two different lengths.
- Reveal: Your tally says it: in this setup (a 30 cm box, 10 seconds), short strings almost never knotted; long ones knotted about half the time. Longer tumbling or a floppier string knots more. A knot forms when a free end weaves through a coil, and a longer string has more coils to weave through. When you put a cord away, clip the two ends together: a closed loop cannot tie itself into a new knot (that follows from knot theory; the experiment did not test clipped strings).
- Claims a learner will see, with planned sources: (1) In the standard setup (0.30 m cubic box, 1 rev/s, 10 s), strings shorter than about 0.46 m almost never knotted; knotting probability rose sharply from 0.46 to 1.5 m and levelled off near 50% from 1.5 to 6 m (Raymer & Smith 2007, "Spontaneous knotting of an agitated string", *PNAS* 104(42), 16432–16437, DOI 10.1073/pnas.0611320104). The setup is always stated with the figure; "long cords knot about half the time" without it is wrong (the paper reports up to 74% for longer tumbling and approaching 100% for long, flexible strings). (2) Stiffer strings knotted less, and longer tumbling knotted more (same source; include only if the stage shows it; the reveal's one line on it uses this claim). (3) A knot forms when a free end passes through a loop (same source, the random braid-move model at the string's end). (4) A closed loop cannot change its knot type without passing through itself, so a cord with its ends joined cannot form a new true knot; it can still bunch up. This is a mathematical consequence, not an experimental result (Adams, *The Knot Book*, W.H. Freeman 1994, AMS reprint 2004, ch. 1 "Introduction", the definitions of knot and knot type; the builder gives the page and the fact-checker confirms it). (5) Each tumble on the stage is a seeded random draw from the published probability curve, read off the paper's Figure 2, approximately (disclosed, pattern `disclose-chosen-seeds`; values re-read from the full text at verification).
- Nearest lessons: probability lessons on simulation runs share the format, not the idea; nothing on knots.
- Format: Simulation run (seeded, tallies; the learner's own run is quoted in the reveal).
- Safety class: none (confirmed by the safety review; the hero shows a plain cord or string, not a powered cable; clipping ends is framed as storage only).
- Illustrations: hero 1:1 (request `long-cord-knots-itself`).
