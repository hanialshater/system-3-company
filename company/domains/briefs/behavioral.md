## Rules every brief below follows (charter; neighbour conditions)
1. **The strongest claim is about the learner's own answers** (types a and b). The reveal never calls a departure "wrong"; it says what the stated model cannot hold.
2. **No sentence about other people and no verdict word** ("holds up", "holds with limits", ...) reaches the learner until its W-902 line is sourced and opened (G0 condition 3). At most one held type (d) sentence per reveal, and the verdict counts as one (G1 condition 2). Every other type (d) sentence is cut, not held.
3. **No failed effect is named**, not even as a contrast (G0 condition 4). No stage sets two normative decision rules against each other (G0 condition 1).
4. **No reveal re-states a verified Economics claim**; it links the lesson (E0). A probability or EV may be stated on screen; no predict question asks the learner to compute or estimate one (P0 condition 3).
5. **The within-learner cost is said**, as a "Where it stops" line about this stage, not as a claim about other people.

### 1. sure-million-or-long-shot · concept: allais-certainty-effect · core · demand 3
- Format: Single card (two choice rows, one toggle). Review route: standard (fact-checker, learner-evaluator). No interaction-designer spec needed: default format, ≤ 6 controls.
- Learner knows: expected value and that a cautious person may prefer a sure amount to a gamble (prerequisites `probability:expected-value`, `economics:risk-aversion`). / does not know: that expected utility, with **any** increasing utility function, ties two choices together when they share a common chance, so "being cautious" cannot explain taking the sure thing in one pair and the long shot in the other.
- Misconception: "Taking the sure $1M in one pair and the 10% chance of $5M in the other just means I am cautious; some utility function fits that."
- Stated model: expected utility with any increasing u (P0 condition 1), named on the stage as "the expected-utility model". Not expected value: the reveal never says a choice is worse because the other option has a higher EV (P1 condition 1). No EV is shown on the cards.
- Prediction (stage text, 18 words by script): "Someone takes the sure $1M in pair 1. Under expected utility, which may they choose in pair 2?" options: A) Only the 11% chance of $1M / B) Only the 10% chance of $5M / C) Either one, depending on how cautious they are (answer: A). Check script: EU(1A) − EU(1B) and EU(2A) − EU(2B) have identical coefficients (0.11·u($1M) − 0.10·u($5M) − 0.01·u(0)); 20,058 increasing utility functions (CRRA, CARA, 20,000 seeded random) all pick the same letter in both pairs, none a mixed pair.
- What the stage shows: two rows of gamble cards, each card a 100-cell bar split by outcome. Pair 1: **1A** $1M for sure / **1B** 10% $5M, 89% $1M, 1% nothing. Pair 2: **2A** 11% $1M, 89% nothing / **2B** 10% $5M, 90% nothing. Locked stage: both pairs visible, no choice active. Controls in DOM order: pair-1 choice (1A | 1B; milestone `pair1`), pair-2 choice (2A | 2B; milestone `pair2`), toggle "Take away the shared 89%" (greys the 89-cell block that both cards in a pair share, $1M in pair 1 and nothing in pair 2, and lines the two pairs up as the same 11-against-10-and-1 comparison; milestone `shared`). Reveal unlocks after all three. The learner's two choices stay marked on the cards when the reveal opens.
- Labels: card outcomes 12 px; bar cell legend 11 px; toggle label 12 px.
- Reveal (revised 2026-10-15 on the learning designer's note, `company/reviews/sure-million-or-long-shot/design.md`, accepted by cm-behavioral; one of four branches by the learner's choices; 75-85 words by script):
  - 1A + 2B: "You took the sure $1M, then the 10% chance of $5M. Take away the 89% each pair shares, and both pairs become 11% of $1M against 10% of $5M. Expected utility must answer it the same way both times, so no utility function fits your two choices: a departure from the model, not a slip. This lean to the sure thing is the certainty effect. Where it stops: you chose knowing the pairs were linked." (75 words)
  - 1B + 2A: the same, opening "You took the 10% chance of $5M, then the 11% chance of $1M." and with "Leaning away from the sure thing is the reverse of the certainty effect." (80 words)
  - 1A + 2A / 1B + 2B: "... You answered it the same way both times, so one utility function fits your two choices: a cautious one, for which $1M is worth more than 10/11 of $5M" / "a bolder one, for which $5M is worth more than 1.1 times $1M". Both add: "The sure $1M, then the 10% chance of $5M, would fit none: that is the certainty effect." (85 and 83 words)
  - The script prints all four branches in full; the builder copies them, not this summary. The builder keeps the "shared 89%" toggle on once reached, so the picture the reveal quotes is on screen (design note).
- Claims:
  - Derivation (a): (1) the two differences in expected utility are the same expression, so 1A over 1B if and only if 2A over 2B, for any u; (2) removing the shared 89% leaves 11% of $1M against 10% of $5M (and 1% of nothing) in both pairs; (3) with u(0) = 0, 1A and 2A fit iff u($1M) > 10/11·u($5M), 1B and 2B iff u($5M) > 1.1·u($1M); witnesses 1 − e^(−3x) and √x. Support `derivation`, script `behavioral-allais-certainty-effect.mjs`.
  - The learner's own screen (b): (4) "no utility function fits both of your choices" (mixed pairs) / "one utility function fits both" (matched pairs), conditional on the learner's two choices; all four combinations in the script. The walkthrough must show each branch.
  - Convention: (5) "the certainty effect" is the name of the departure (a term, not an empirical claim about people).
  - **Source, held (the one type (d) line; mixed branches only):** (6) 1A + 2B adds "Re-run with 4,098 people in 19 countries, it held up, weaker than first reported." (89 words); 1B + 2A replaces its naming sentence with "That is the reverse of the certainty effect, which held up with 4,098 people in 19 countries, weaker than first reported." (88 words). Source: W-902 row "Allais / certainty effect" (robust, smaller than the 1979 original): Ruggeri et al. 2020 **as reported by** Cicerale et al. 2022, *PLoS ONE*, PMC9337702 (opened by research-lead; Ruggeri 2020 itself is not in PMC). "Weaker than first reported" is the limit, in the same sentence, so it stays one held line (G1 condition 2). Whether a second-hand attribution is enough is the **fact-checker's** call. **Fallback if not checkable: the sentence is cut and the unheld text stands.** The lesson teaches fully without it.
  - **Cut, not held** (G1 condition 2): "how often people choose 1A and 2B". No attribution line ("Allais, 1953") in learner text, because it would be a second type (d) line.
- Neighbour conditions this brief meets: E0 (no Economics claim re-stated; `square-root-wealth` linked), E1 (no certainty equivalent computed), P0 (expected utility as the model; no predict asks for a probability or EV), P1 (the reveal is the inconsistency across the pair; EV only as a linked prerequisite; no spread-of-outcomes or run-of-plays stage), G0 (no failed effect named; no two normative rules set against each other), G1 (one held sentence, fallback written).
- Duplicate search (terms: Allais, certainty effect, common consequence, independence axiom, money pump): 0 lessons, 0 briefs except the money pump in `math.discrete.md` (`transitive-preferences`). Nearest: `economics/square-root-wealth` ("A sure amount versus a coin", concept `risk-aversion`) fits **one** concave function to **one** gamble; this lesson shows that **no** function fits **two** choices, so its wrong answer ("caution explains both") is not that lesson's ("indifferent means worth $50"). `math:transitive-preferences` pumps a cycle of preferences for money; this one has no cycle, only two pairs that one model ties together.

### 2. same-week-a-year-apart · concept: present-bias-reversal · core · demand 3
- Format: Single card (one timeline with a start-date slider, two choice rows). Review route: standard. No interaction-designer spec needed.
- Learner knows: that money later can be worth less than money now (prerequisite `economics:present-value`, lesson `wait-or-take-now`). / does not know: that under steady (exponential) discounting the choice between two dated amounts depends only on the gap between them, so moving the pair closer cannot change the choice at any rate.
- Misconception: "Taking $100 today but waiting a week for $110 a year from now just means I discount at a high rate; some steady rate fits that."
- Stated model: steady (exponential) discounting, named on the stage as "a simple model: each week counts the same" (E4: named as a simplification). No market rate, no present value and no weekly factor appear on screen (E4).
- Prediction (stage text, 24 words by script; "Moved to today" replaced on the learning designer's note, because it left unclear what moved): "Under steady discounting, someone prefers $110 at week 53 to $100 at week 52. Offered $100 today or $110 in a week, they must:" options: A) Wait a week again for the $110 / B) Take the $100, since today weighs more / C) Either, depending on their discount rate (answer: A). Check script: the later-to-sooner value ratio is 1.1·d at every start date (max deviation 4.4e-16 over d in 0.5-1.2), so every steady rate gives one choice at all three slider stops; the same holds in continuous time.
- What the stage shows: a timeline from today to week 53 with the pair drawn as two coins, "$100 at week t" and "$110 at week t + 1". Locked stage: the pair at t = 52; slider and choices disabled. Controls in DOM order: far choice ($100 at week 52 | $110 at week 53; milestone `far`), start-date slider with **three stops, 52, 13 and 0 weeks** (ruling 2026-10-15 on the designer's QA-path flaw: the slider value is closeness, so ArrowRight moves the pair toward today and the QA path's two presses land on today; six stops never reached today). The pair slides with a "1 week, +$10" gap bracket drawn the same at every stop (designer's addition, accepted), and a strip under the timeline shows the steady-model answer implied by the far choice, the same at every stop; milestone `slide` at 0, near choice ($100 today | $110 in a week; milestone `near`, enabled only at stop 0). Reveal unlocks after all three. Both choices stay marked on the timeline when the reveal opens.
- Labels: coin amounts 12 px; week ticks 11 px; model strip 11 px.
- Reveal (revised 2026-10-15 on the learning designer's note, `company/reviews/same-week-a-year-apart/design.md`, accepted; one of four branches; 86-89 words by script with the held limit L, 75-78 without):
  - waited a year out, took today: "A year out you waited a week for the $110; today you took the $100. Steady discounting judges the pair only by its gap, one week for $10 more, and the gap never changed as the pair moved to today. No steady discount rate fits both of your choices. A model that gives today extra weight does: that departure is called present bias. [L] Where it stops: imagined money, chosen once each, with your first choice in view." (88 words with L)
  - took a year out, waited today: "... Yours switched toward patience as the date came closer, the reverse of present bias. [L] ..." (88)
  - the same both times: "Both a year out and today you ... One steady rate fits both of your choices. Switching to the $100 today would fit only a model that gives today extra weight, called present bias. [L] ..." (89 and 86)
  - L, in every branch, because every branch names present bias: "With real payments, reversals are clearer for effort than for money." "Imagined payments" became "imagined money", so the stage's own limit names money.
  - The script prints all four in full.
- Claims:
  - Derivation (a): (1) under exponential discounting the ratio of the $110's value to the $100's is 1.1·d whatever the start date, so the choice is the same at every stop; (2) the same in continuous time; (3) a model that gives today extra weight (quasi-hyperbolic, witness b = 0.9, d = 1) can wait a year out and take the $100 today. Support `derivation`, script `behavioral-present-bias-reversal.mjs`.
  - The learner's own screen (b): (4) "no steady discount rate fits both of your choices" (switched) / "one steady rate fits both" (same), conditional on the two choices; all four combinations in the script.
  - Convention: (5) "present bias" is the name of the departure.
  - **Source, held (the one type (d) line, the limit):** (6) L, "With real payments, reversals are clearer for effort than for money." Source: W-902 row "Present bias" (with limits): Augenblick, Niederle & Sprenger 2015 **as reported by** Cohen, Ericson, Laibson & White 2020, *J Econ Lit*, PMC10489299 (opened by research-lead): with money the inconsistency was "much weaker (but still statistically significant)". "With real payments" stays, because Ruggeri et al. 2022 (PMC9584811) found present bias in unpaid monetary choices in all 61 countries. Whether the second-hand route is enough is the **fact-checker's** call.
  - **Ruling (2026-10-15, designer's open question 2): if L cannot be verified, the lesson still names present bias.** L is cut; nothing replaces it. Reasons: "present bias" is a term for the learner's own switch (convention, claim 5), not a claim that other people do it, and with L cut the reveal has no sentence about other people and no verdict word, as G0 condition 3 requires. The replication rule's "with limits" tier binds claims about people, and the cut lesson makes none. The stage's own limit stays in every branch: "Where it stops: imagined money, chosen once each, with your first choice in view." ### 4. saved-or-die-same-600 · concept: gain-loss-framing · intro · demand 3
- Format: Single card (two choice rows, one toggle). Review route: standard (the outbreak is hypothetical and unnamed; no real disease, country or group). No interaction-designer spec needed: default format, ≤ 6 controls.

### 5. same-fifty-two-labels · concept: mental-accounting · intro · demand 2
- Format: Single card (two yes/no rows, one toggle). Review route: standard. No interaction-designer spec needed.
- Learner knows: that money spent one way cannot be spent another (prerequisite `economics:opportunity-cost`); has met `economics:sunk-cost` (prerequisite in the curriculum; this lesson does not use it). / does not know: that a label on money still in hand does not change what it can buy, so two people with the same money and bills can afford the same things whatever their labels say.
- Misconception: "Money labelled for one use is not available for another." Not "money already spent should count" (E6 point 2): nothing on this stage is spent, paid in advance or lost.
- Stated model: fungibility: "the same money and the same bills buy the same things". No rate, discount, "true cost" or budget line (E6 point 4, change 2).
