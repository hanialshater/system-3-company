### N1. new-road-slower-trip · concept: braess-paradox · core · demand 2
curriculum.references: easley-kleinberg-2010 ch. 8 §8.1–8.2 (§ unconfirmed)
- Learner knows: that a road's trip time grows with traffic, simple division / does not know: that adding a free road can raise the trip time for everyone, because each driver picks the fastest route for themselves.
- Misconception: "Adding a road never makes things worse. At worst nobody uses it."
- Prediction: "4,000 drivers cross a town. Each route is a 'x/100' road (x = cars on it, minutes) plus a fixed 45-minute road, so today every trip takes 65 minutes. A free 0-minute link joins the two middles and every driver takes the quickest route. How long does a trip take?" options: A) about 40 min / B) 65 min, unchanged / C) 80 min (answer: C).
- Node check (re-run by me, 2026-10-14): network S->A (x/100), A->End (45), S->B (45), B->End (x/100), new A->B (0). Before: 2,000 per route, 2,000/100 + 45 = 65 for both. After: with s drivers on S-A-B-End and the rest split evenly over the two old routes, the new route takes 20 + s/100 and each old route takes 65 + s/200. The new route is faster for every s from 0 to 4,000 (it is faster while s < 9,000), so the only equilibrium is s = 4,000 (checked for every integer s). Then the new route takes 40 + 0 + 40 = 80. One driver who goes back to an old route takes 4,000/100 + 45 = 85, so nobody gains by leaving. Everyone loses 15 minutes (65 -> 80). A planner could do slightly better than 65 (best average 64.69 at 500 on the new link), so do not say "the road should be closed" as a rule; say only that the selfish equilibrium is 80. Caveat: this holds for these numbers, not for every network.
- Interaction (before/after toggle plus one slider): stage = the diamond graph, 4 labelled roads, a before/after toggle, and a slider "drivers on the new link" (0 to 4,000 in steps of 500). Readouts: "new link: N min / old route: M min" (stage text under 25 words). Controls exist from the first render. Milestones: the slider at 0 (a lone user saves 25 min: 40 vs 65), at 2,000 (60 vs 75), at 4,000 (80 vs 85, and the toggle "after" now shows 80 for all). Every value the reveal names is reachable by pressing each control once.
- Payoff: each driver's best choice is the new link, so all 4,000 take it, and the result (80) is worse for every one of them than before (65). Quote only reached states: "At 4,000 on the new link the new route takes 80 and the old one 85, so nobody leaves. Before it was 65." One sentence that it is a model with these numbers.
- Planned claims (support, source): (1) before the link every trip takes 65 min (arithmetic from the rules shown; seeded none, deterministic). (2) With the link, the equilibrium has all 4,000 on the new route, 80 min each (node enumeration over every s; the check script goes into the meta). (3) One driver switching back would take 85 min (arithmetic). (4) The example network is the book's, Easley and Kleinberg 2010, ch. 8 (source: the book; **§ unconfirmed**). (5) Braess, D. (1968), "Über ein Paradoxon aus der Verkehrsplanung", Unternehmensforschung 12: 258-268 (from memory, fact-checker confirms the citation or drops it). No empirical real-road claim in this lesson; if a real example (the book mentions Seoul and New York cases) is wanted it is a separate, human-source-checked claim and is out of this brief.
- Format: Before / after toggle (company/design/formats.md; 🟡 by hand). **Needs an interaction-designer spec**: the toggle plus slider on a diamond graph at 390 px with four road labels.
- Illustrations: hero 1:1 during predict only (≤ 96x96, top right, not over a road label): a bridge or tunnel mouth. Reveal: before/after pair (4:3), two frames ≤ 160 px wide: the diamond without and with the middle link. No picture beside the live diagram.
- Notes: game framing to be checked by cm-economics-markets (review record objection 4); if that CM prefers to own the build, swap, no approval needed. Rank in strand: 1 (no cs.systems brief ahead of it).

### N2. web-bow-tie-reach · concept: web-bow-tie · core · demand 2
curriculum.references: easley-kleinberg-2010 ch. 13 §13.3–13.4 (§ unconfirmed)
- Learner knows: that web pages link to other pages, and what "reach by following links" means (BFS lesson wave-from-the-corner) / does not know: that links are one-way, so being connected is not the same as being reachable.
- Misconception: "If two pages are joined by links somewhere, you can click from one to the other. The web is one big connected thing."
- Prediction: "12 small web pages. Ignoring link direction, 10 of them are joined to the central group. You start on page C1 in that group and click links the way they point, as far as you can. How many pages can you reach, counting C1?" options: A) 10 / B) 7 / C) 4 (answer: B).
- Node check (re-run by me, 2026-10-14): the 12-page directed graph, edges: C1->C2, C2->C3, C3->C4, C3->O1, C4->C1, C4->C2, C4->O2, I1->C1, I1->I2, I1->T1, I2->C2, O1->O2, O2->O3, D1->D2. Core {C1..C4} is strongly connected. Reach (counting the start): from C1, C2, C3, C4 it is 7 (the four core pages plus O1, O2, O3); from I1 10; from I2 8; from O1 3; O2 2; O3 1; T1 1; D1 2; D2 1. Ignoring direction, the component of C1 has 10 pages (the core, I1, I2, O1-O3, T1), D1-D2 are cut off. Over the 132 ordered pairs of distinct pages, 44 have a click path (one third). The reveal does not use the one-third figure unless the stage lets the learner reach it (it does not), so it stays in the meta as an extra check only.
- Interaction (single card, directed graph, tap-a-start): 12 nodes, drawn as a bow-tie (IN left, core centre, OUT right, one tendril, a detached pair), arrows with heads. Tap any page and the reachable set fills and the count shows ("reach 7 of 12"). Stage text under 25 words. Legend by shape and fill (not colour alone). Milestones: tapped a core page (7), tapped an IN page (I1: 10), tapped an OUT page (O3: 1).
- Payoff: one-way links split the web into regions: from the core you reach the core and OUT but never IN. From an OUT page you get almost nowhere. Quote only tapped states: "From C1 you reach 7 of 12. From I1 you reach 10. From O3 only itself." Then: Broder et al. (2000) found the same bow-tie shape in a crawl of about 200 million pages (numbers below are **unverified, needs a human source check**).
- Planned claims (support, source): (1) reach counts in the toy graph are 7 / 10 / 8 / 3 / 2 / 1 / 1 / 2 / 1 (node BFS, script in the meta). (2) Links are directed, so reachability is not symmetric (definition). (3) The web has a bow-tie structure: a strongly connected core, IN, OUT, tendrils and disconnected pieces (Broder, Kumar, Maghoul, Raghavan, Rajagopalan, Stata, Tomkins and Wiener, "Graph structure in the Web", Computer Networks 33, 2000; the book ch. 13; **needs human source check**). (4) In that crawl of about 203 million pages the core was about 28% and IN and OUT about 21% each (from the review record and my memory of the paper; **unverified, needs a human source check**; if the paper's figures differ the reveal drops the percentages). The reveal says the toy graph is not to scale.
- Format: Single card on a small directed graph (default, kit support today). No interaction-designer spec required; it is the vertical's third hand-built network stage, so the design lead's `network-stage` concept (after the rule of two) would apply. The toy graph is 12 nodes, within the review's 12-node bound for the first network lessons.
- Illustrations: hero 1:1 during predict only (≤ 96x96, top right, away from the graph): a bow tie. No reveal pair.
- Notes: not the PageRank lesson (`one-good-link-beats-many`, cs.algorithms), which ranks pages on a graph; this one is about which pages you can reach. Rank in strand: 2.
