### N1. new-road-slower-trip · concept: braess-paradox · core · demand 2
curriculum.references: easley-kleinberg-2010 ch. 8 §8.1–8.2 (§ unconfirmed)
- Learner knows: that a road's trip time grows with traffic, simple division / does not know: that adding a free road can raise the trip time for everyone, because each driver picks the fastest route for themselves.
- Misconception: "Adding a road never makes things worse. At worst nobody uses it."
- Prediction: "4,000 drivers cross a town. Each route is a 'x/100' road (x = cars on it, minutes) plus a fixed 45-minute road, so today every trip takes 65 minutes. A free 0-minute link joins the two middles and every driver takes the quickest route. How long does a trip take?" options: A) about 40 min / B) 65 min, unchanged / C) 80 min (answer: C).
- Node check (re-run by me in node, 2026-10-14; corrected the same day, an earlier text said 20 + s/100 and s < 9,000): network S->A (x/100), A->End (45), S->B (45), B->End (x/100), new A->B (0). Before: 2,000 per route, 2,000/100 + 45 = 65 for both. After: with s drivers on S-A-B-End and the rest split evenly over the two old routes, the new route takes 40 + s/100 and each old route takes 65 + s/200. The new route is faster for every s from 0 to 4,000 (it is faster while s < 5,000), so the only equilibrium is s = 4,000 (checked for every integer s, including uneven splits). Step values: s = 0, 1,000, 2,000, 3,000, 4,000 give new vs old 40/65, 50/70, 60/75, 70/80, 80/85; a step run of 1,000 stops at 4,000 after 4 moves. Then the new route takes 40 + 0 + 40 = 80. One driver who goes back to an old route takes 4,000/100 + 45 = 85, so nobody gains by leaving. Everyone loses 15 minutes (65 -> 80). A planner could do slightly better than 65 (best average 64.69 at 500 on the new link), so do not say "the road should be closed" as a rule; say only that the selfish equilibrium is 80. Caveat: this holds for these numbers, not for every network.
- Interaction (before/after toggle plus step buttons; spec company/design/specs/new-road-slower-trip.md replaced the slider): stage = the diamond graph, 4 named nodes and 5 roads, a Before / After toggle, and two buttons "Switch 1,000" (moves 1,000 drivers onto the new route, 500 from each old route, only while the new route is faster) and "Run" (repeats to the end). Readouts: "On new route N · New route X · Old route Y" (stage text under 25 words). Controls exist from the first render. QA path: After (s = 0, 40 vs 65: a lone user saves 25 min) -> Switch 1,000 (50 vs 70) -> Run (s = 4,000, 80 vs 85; After then shows 80 for all, Before shows 65). Gating milestones are two: link-open (s = 0) and nobody-gains (s = 4,000). The 2,000 state (60 vs 75) is optional, reached by pressing Switch 1,000 twice; the reveal quotes it only if visited. Every value the reveal must name (40, 65, 80, 85) is on the QA path.
- Payoff: each driver's best choice is the new link, so all 4,000 take it, and the result (80) is worse for every one of them than before (65). Quote only reached states: "At 4,000 on the new link the new route takes 80 and the old one 85, so nobody leaves. Before it was 65." One sentence that it is a model with these numbers.
- Planned claims (support, source): (1) before the link every trip takes 65 min (arithmetic from the rules shown; seeded none, deterministic). (2) With the link, the equilibrium has all 4,000 on the new route, 80 min each (node enumeration over every s; the check script goes into the meta). (3) One driver switching back would take 85 min (arithmetic). (4) The example network is the book's, Easley and Kleinberg 2010, ch. 8 (source: the book; **§ unconfirmed**). (5) Braess, D. (1968), "Über ein Paradoxon aus der Verkehrsplanung", Unternehmensforschung 12: 258-268 (from memory, fact-checker confirms the citation or drops it). No empirical real-road claim in this lesson; if a real example (the book mentions Seoul and New York cases) is wanted it is a separate, human-source-checked claim and is out of this brief.
- Format: Before / after toggle (company/design/formats.md; 🟡 by hand). **Needs an interaction-designer spec**: the toggle plus slider on a diamond graph at 390 px with four road labels.
- Illustrations: hero 1:1 during predict only (≤ 96x96, top right, not over a road label): a bridge or tunnel mouth. Reveal: before/after pair (4:3), two frames ≤ 160 px wide: the diamond without and with the middle link. No picture beside the live diagram.

### N2. web-bow-tie-reach · concept: web-bow-tie · core · demand 2
curriculum.references: easley-kleinberg-2010 ch. 13 §13.3–13.4 (§ unconfirmed)
- Learner knows: that web pages link to other pages, and what "reach by following links" means (BFS lesson wave-from-the-corner) / does not know: that links are one-way, so being connected is not the same as being reachable.
- Misconception: "If two pages are joined by links somewhere, you can click from one to the other. The web is one big connected thing."
- Prediction: "12 small web pages. Ignoring link direction, 10 of them are joined to the central group. You start on page C1 in that group and click links the way they point, as far as you can. How many pages can you reach, counting C1?" options: A) 10 / B) 7 / C) 4 (answer: B).
- Node check (re-run by me, 2026-10-14): the 12-page directed graph, edges: C1->C2, C2->C3, C3->C4, C3->O1, C4->C1, C4->C2, C4->O2, I1->C1, I1->I2, I1->T1, I2->C2, O1->O2, O2->O3, D1->D2. Core {C1..C4} is strongly connected. Reach (counting the start): from C1, C2, C3, C4 it is 7 (the four core pages plus O1, O2, O3); from I1 10; from I2 8; from O1 3; O2 2; O3 1; T1 1; D1 2; D2 1. Ignoring direction, the component of C1 has 10 pages (the core, I1, I2, O1-O3, T1), D1-D2 are cut off. Over the 132 ordered pairs of distinct pages, 44 have a click path (one third). The reveal does not use the one-third figure unless the stage lets the learner reach it (it does not), so it stays in the meta as an extra check only.
- Interaction (single card, directed graph, tap-a-start): 12 nodes, drawn as a bow-tie (IN left, core centre, OUT right, one tendril, a detached pair), arrows with heads. Tap any page and the reachable set fills and the count shows ("reach 7 of 12"). Stage text under 25 words. Legend by shape and fill (not colour alone). Milestones: tapped a core page (7), tapped an IN page (I1: 10), tapped an OUT page (O3: 1).
- Payoff: one-way links split the web into regions: from the core you reach the core and OUT but never IN. From an OUT page you get almost nowhere. Quote only tapped states: "From C1 you reach 7 of 12. From I1 you reach 10. From O3 only itself." Then: Broder et al. (2000) found the same bow-tie shape in a crawl of about 200 million pages (numbers below are **unverified, needs a human source check**).
- Planned claims (support, source): (1) reach counts in the toy graph are 7 / 10 / 8 / 3 / 2 / 1 / 1 / 2 / 1 (node BFS, script in the meta). (2) Links are directed, so reachability is not symmetric (definition). (3) The web has a bow-tie structure: a strongly connected core, IN, OUT, tendrils and disconnected pieces (Broder, Kumar, Maghoul, Raghavan, Rajagopalan, Stata, Tomkins and Wiener, "Graph structure in the Web", Computer Networks 33, 2000; the book ch. 13; **needs human source check**). (4) In that crawl of about 203 million pages the core was about 28% and IN and OUT about 21% each (from the review record and my memory of the paper; **unverified, needs a human source check**; if the paper's figures differ the reveal drops the percentages). The reveal says the toy graph is not to scale.
- Format: Single card on a small directed graph (default, kit support today). No interaction-designer spec required; it is the vertical's third hand-built network stage, so the design lead's `network-stage` concept (after the rule of two) would apply. The toy graph is 12 nodes, within the review's 12-node bound for the first network lessons.
- Illustrations: hero 1:1 during predict only (≤ 96x96, top right, away from the graph): a bow tie. No reveal pair.
- Notes: not the PageRank lesson (`one-good-link-beats-many`, cs.algorithms), which ranks pages on a graph; this one is about which pages you can reach. Rank in strand: 2.

#### 1. selfish-routes-cost-a-third-more · concept: cs:price-of-anarchy · core · demand 2 · rank 11 · format: Single card (split slider drives live chart)
- Sections: ch. 8 · domain: cs
- Audience: students in a networks course and curious adults (vertical audience). Learner knows: drivers pick the faster road (traffic equilibrium) / does not know: that the selfish equilibrium can be worse on average than a planner's split, by up to 4/3 with roads whose delay grows in proportion to traffic.
- Misconception: If every driver picks the fastest route, the average trip is as short as it can be.
- Prediction (stage text ≤ 25 words): "Road A always takes 60 min. Road B takes 60 min × its share of drivers. Selfish drivers average 60. Can a planner beat that?" options: A. No, 60 is best / B. Yes, to 45 / C. Yes, to 30 (answer: B (half on each: 45 min)).

- Reveal (one line): Everyone piles onto B because it is never slower than A, which makes it 60 min; sending half to A cuts B to 30 and the average to 45.
- Planned claims:
  1. With everyone on road B the trip takes 60 minutes on both roads, and no driver gains by switching. support: derivation; source: node.
  2. Splitting drivers half and half gives an average of 45 minutes, the minimum. support: derivation; source: node grid search.
  3. With delays that grow in proportion to traffic, the selfish equilibrium's total travel time is at most 4/3 of the best possible. support: citation (theorem); source: E&K ch. 8; Roughgarden and Tardos (2002), J. ACM 49(2); Pigou (1920) example. Attribution: fact-checker confirms.

### 8. one-link-back-grows-the-core · concept: cs:core-grows-by-one-link · core · demand 2 · format: Single card (hand-drawn network stage, 10 pages; link preset chips)

### 9. drivers-stop-switching · concept: cs:best-response-convergence · core · demand 2 · format: Single card with step-run button (switch log and delay meter)

- Learner knows: each driver picks the faster road (new-road-slower-trip, selfish-routes-cost-a-third-more) / does not know: that when drivers switch one at a time, a meter of total delay falls by exactly each switcher's saving, so the switching must stop.
- Misconception: Selfish drivers chasing the faster road keep flipping back and forth for ever.
- Prediction (stage text ≤ 25 words): "Six drivers, two roads. One at a time, a driver switches if the other road is faster for them right now. The switching:" options: A. All switching at once from all-on-B alternates 55 min (all on B) and 60 min (all on A) for ever (hand check: 10 < 55, then 30 < 60).

### 3. same-sum-different-speed · concept: cache-locality · core · demand 3
- Format: Side-by-side comparison
- Audience and knowledge boundary: learner knows: a small cache keeps recently used items and evicts the least recently used (lru-eviction-order) / does not know: that memory arrives in lines of neighbouring cells, so the order you visit data decides how many slow fetches you pay.
- Misconception: Adding up the same 64 numbers costs the same whatever order you visit them in.
- Prediction (stage text, 23 words): "An 8×8 grid is stored row by row. Each fetch brings 4 neighbouring cells; 2 fetches stay nearby. Rows take 16 fetches. Columns?" options: A) 16 / B) 32 / C) 64 (answer: C). The locked stage shows both grids, no fetch counts.
- Check (node, 2026-10-14): model: cell (r, c) is at r × 8 + c; a line is 4 consecutive cells (16 lines); the near memory keeps 2 lines, evicting the least recently used. Row order: 16 fetches. Column order: 64 (each step down a column is 8 cells on, in a new line, and the 2 kept lines are gone before the next column returns to them). Column order with 8 lines kept: 16; with 7 kept: 64.
- What the stage shows: two 8 x 8 grids side by side (cells 18 px; each grid 144 px), "rows" and "columns"; each fetched line flashes as a 4-cell bar; fetch counters under each grid. Controls in DOM order: Add by rows (16), Add by columns (64), Columns, keep 8 lines (16), Reset (4). Each run completes at once (final counts shown; a Step button is optional after Reset). A trace keeps the three counts.
- Key state (milestones): rows (16), columns (64), columns-kept (16). Reveal unlocks after all three.
- Reveal payoff (draft, 84 words): "Row by row, each fetch brought 4 cells you used next, so 64 cells cost 16 fetches. Column by column, the next cell was 8 cells further on, in a different line, and the 2 kept lines were pushed out before you came back: 64 fetches, four times as many, for the same sum. When 8 lines could stay nearby, columns also needed only 16. Processors fetch memory in lines like this, which is why swapping two loops can make a program much faster."
- Phone fit (390x844): the reveal points at the two grids' counters and the trace. Two 144 px grids fit side by side at 390 px with labels >= 10 px; stage-top crop 0 px.
- Planned claims:
  1. In the model, row order costs 16 fetches and column order 64; column order with 8 kept lines costs 16 (with 7, 64). support: simulation; source: node (fact-checker can derive: rows touch each of 16 lines once; a column touches 8 lines, more than 2 kept).
  2. Processors move memory between main memory and cache in fixed-size blocks (cache lines), so nearby accesses are cheaper. support: citation; source: Hennessy and Patterson, Computer Architecture: A Quantitative Approach, memory-hierarchy chapter (spatial locality; loop interchange as a compiler optimisation). Attribution: fact-checker confirms from the book; the reveal gives no real-hardware speed-up number.
- Curriculum (new concept): `{"id":"cache-locality","name":"Cache lines and locality: visit order changes cost","level":"core","demand":3,"prerequisites":["lru-cache"],"subarea":"systems"}`; also none; references none; tags memory, cache, locality.
- Differs from nearest: lru-eviction-order (lru-cache) asks which item an LRU cache evicts; this asks how the visit order and line size decide the fetch count. more-memory-more-misses (brief 6 below) compares eviction rules; build this first.

### 4. eight-cores-not-eight-times · concept: amdahls-law · intro · demand 3
- Format: Single card
- Audience and knowledge boundary: learner knows: splitting work among helpers saves time / does not know: that the part that cannot be split sets a hard limit, so 8 cores give far less than 8 times the speed.
- Misconception: With 8 cores a job runs 8 times faster.
- Prediction (stage text, 21 words): "A 60-minute job: 45 minutes can be split across cores, 15 minutes cannot. With 8 cores, how long does it take?" options: A) 7.5 min / B) About 21 min / C) 30 min (answer: B, 20.6). The locked stage shows the job bar at 1 core, unlabelled.
- Check (derivation, node, 2026-10-14): time = 15 + 45 / cores: 1 core 60 (1x), 2 cores 37.5 (1.6x), 4 cores 26.25 (2.29x), 8 cores 20.6 (2.91x), unlimited 15 (4x). Model: the splittable part divides perfectly, no overhead (caption).
- What the stage shows: one job bar: a grey 15-minute block that cannot split, and the 45-minute part drawn as stacked lanes, one per core; readouts "time" and "speed-up". Controls in DOM order: 2 cores, 4 cores, 8 cores, Unlimited cores, Reset (5). A trace keeps each visited setting's time.
- Key state (milestones): question-case (8 cores: 20.6 min, 2.91x), limit (unlimited: 15 min, 4x). Reveal unlocks after both (the QA path visits 2 and 4 on the way).
- Reveal payoff (draft, 68 words): "With 8 cores the 45 splittable minutes shrank to under 6, but the 15 that cannot be split stayed 15: 20.6 minutes, 2.9 times faster, not 8. Unlimited cores still left the 15 minutes, so this job can never run more than 4 times faster. The part that must run on one core sets the limit. This is Amdahl's law: more cores help only the part that splits."
- Phone fit (390x844): the reveal points at the grey block and the trace. One bar, two readouts, one trace; stage-top crop 0 px.
- Planned claims:
  1. 15 + 45/8 = 20.6 minutes, a speed-up of 2.91. support: derivation.
  2. With unlimited cores the time approaches 15 minutes, a speed-up of at most 4. support: derivation.
  3. The limit is Amdahl's law: speed-up = 1 / (serial share + parallel share / cores). support: citation + derivation; source: Amdahl (1967), "Validity of the single processor approach to achieving large scale computing capabilities", AFIPS Conference Proceedings 30; Hennessy and Patterson (any edition, quantitative principles chapter). Attribution: fact-checker confirms.
  4. The model ignores the cost of splitting and coordinating work (caption). support: definition.

### 5. faster-link-same-wait · concept: latency-vs-bandwidth · core · demand 3
- Format: Single card
