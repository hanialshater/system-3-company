# People & Places briefs (cm-geography-people-places)

Ranked briefs for this strand, in the same format as `geography.md` (`### N. slug · concept: <id> · level · demand N`). `tools/claim.mjs next geography` hands these out after the domain's own list. Check every brief's answer in node before adding it.

Added 2026-10-13 at the founder's request. Sensitive topic: race and housing. Cultural review required (cultural-reviewer) before build and before publish. Language follows the sources: "groups" (the Census and Brown University use race and Hispanic-origin categories); no stereotypes, no claim about why any person lives anywhere. Overlap: nearest existing lesson is `population-distribution` (where people live is uneven); this one is about sorting, not density. Prerequisite lesson: `mild-preferences-sharp-divides` (Economics, markets), which teaches the mechanism.

### 1. schelling-on-new-york · concept: segregation-model-nyc · core · demand 3 · cultural review required · sensitive topic: race and housing
- Learner knows: the Schelling mechanism (mild preferences, sorted grid) from the Economics lesson / does not know: how the pattern compares with a real city, and what a model like this can and cannot say about it.
- Misconception: "If a simple model reproduces a city's pattern, then that is why the city looks that way."
- Prediction: "Run the same rule on a grid shaped like New York's five boroughs, started from NYC's overall group shares, everyone at random. With a 30% threshold, will the model's segregation come out above or below the real city's measured level?" options: A) Well below / B) About the same / C) Well above (answer to fix after the node check below; do not publish this card without it). Knowledge boundary: a grid of squares is not tracts; the model has no income, no prices, no jobs, no history.
- Check still owed (node): rasterise, run seeds 1 to 5, compute the dissimilarity index (D) on blocks of cells of a stated size, compare with the published D. Generic-grid run from the Economics brief gives like-neighbour share about 0.75 at 30%, but D on a masked NYC grid has not been computed, and D depends on block size, so a model-vs-real comparison may only be fair as "same order, same direction". The brief is not final until that is computed. If the model's D is far from the real one, the card says so rather than tuning the threshold until it matches.
- Interaction: threshold slider, Run / Step / Reset, seeded RNG disclosed. Stage: the NYC-shaped grid, live share happy, and D over ticks with the real published D drawn as a reference line. A final step (toggle, not a second card) reveals the real-world reference line labelled with its source and year.
- Reveal (written to fit 90 words): the model gets to a divided city from mild preferences, so preferences alone can produce the pattern. It does not show that they did. Documented causes include the 1938 HOLC maps, housing and lending discrimination, and income. The learner's own D is quoted next to the real one.
- NYC mask (build prerequisite, not done): rasterise offline from the public NYC Open Data "Borough Boundaries" polygons (NYC Department of City Planning, dataset tqmj-j8zm, https://data.cityofnewyork.us/City-Government/Borough-Boundaries/tqmj-j8zm) to a coarse grid (for example 32 x 32), store as a bitmask of at most 1 KB, and put the dataset, access date and rasterisation script in the claims. No runtime fetch. The Natural Earth 1:110m files are too coarse for NYC.
- Sources to cite (I could not fetch any in this session: outbound access to these sites was blocked, so every figure below is unverified until the fact-checker reads the page):
  - Group shares: US Census Bureau, 2020 Census redistricting data (P.L. 94-171), New York city. Also NYC Department of City Planning 2020 Census page, https://www.nyc.gov/site/planning/planning-level/nyc-population/2020-census.page. From memory only: total 8,804,190; roughly 31% White non-Hispanic, 28% Hispanic, 20% Black non-Hispanic, 16% Asian non-Hispanic. Use these as the model's start shares only after verifying; round, and name the categories as the Census does.
  - Measured segregation: Brown University, US2010 / Diversity and Disparities project, https://s4.ad.brown.edu/projects/diversity/Data/Report/report.asp (dissimilarity indices by metro area and city, 1980 to 2010; reference value D to be read off the page, not recalled). Alternative: NYU Furman Center, State of New York City's Housing and Neighborhoods, https://furmancenter.org/stateofthecity (check whether it carries a dissimilarity index; if only Brown has it, cite Brown). Note: the Brown series ends at 2010 or 2020; if no 2020 D is found, the card says "measured in 20XX" and does not mix years silently.
  - HOLC: Mapping Inequality, University of Richmond Digital Scholarship Lab, https://dsl.richmond.edu/panorama/redlining/ (the 1938 Home Owners' Loan Corporation maps of New York).
  - Other causes: a government or peer-reviewed source on housing and lending discrimination in New York (for example HUD or the Fair Housing Act history; Rothstein 2017, The Color of Law); a source for the income link (Furman Center). Pick one primary source per claim.
- Planned claims with support: (1) NYC had 8,804,190 residents in 2020 (source: Census; unverified until read). (2) The shares used for the start are as published, rounded (Census; unverified). (3) The real dissimilarity index is X in year Y between groups G1 and G2 (Brown; value and years to be read from the source; not given here). (4) D means the share of one group that would have to move for an even spread across tracts, (definition; cite Brown's glossary or Massey and Denton 1988). (5) In the model, with the stated rule and seed, D reaches Z (simulation, seeded). (6) Preferences alone can produce a sorted pattern in the model (simulation; Schelling 1971). (7) This does not show that preferences caused New York's pattern (reasoning claim; mark as judgement). (8) Documented other causes: HOLC maps of 1938 graded neighbourhoods and are associated with later lending patterns (Mapping Inequality, plus one peer-reviewed study to be named by the fact-checker); housing and lending discrimination; income (sources above). (9) The grid mask comes from NYC Open Data borough boundaries, rasterised offline (Dataset page; script in the repo).
- Hard limits for the builder: use "groups" as the sources do; never describe a borough or neighbourhood by a stereotype; no colours that imply good or bad (use two neutral hues, plus a pattern for colour-blind safety); no claim about why a specific neighbourhood is as it is; the reveal must carry the "model, not cause" limit, not a footnote.
- Format: Agent-based simulation (new, proposed). Illustrations: none.
