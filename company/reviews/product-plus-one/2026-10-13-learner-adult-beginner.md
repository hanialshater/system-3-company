# Learner review: product-plus-one
lesson version: v1
boundary: an adult who remembers basic school arithmetic and everyday life; has never studied the topic (knows what a prime is roughly; has not heard Euclid's argument).

## 1. From the artifact

- Prediction: a real guess for the wrong reasons. 30031 cannot be checked by hand, and the options mix two different ideas ("it is prime", "it divides by one of 2 to 13", "it has a prime factor not in the list"). A beginner picks "it is prime" because a number that looks like that usually feels prime, and it is a fair guess. The hook asks a bigger question ("Could a long enough list hold them all?") that the prediction does not ask, so the learner is predicting a property of one number without knowing why it matters.
- Where understanding first broke: step 6, "30,030 + 1 = 30,031 / ITS PRIMES 59 509 / composite: none in the list / Each listed prime leaves remainder 1." The line "Each listed prime leaves remainder 1" is shown on every step, but the stage never shows a single remainder (30031 divided by 2, by 3, ...). It is a caption, not something I can see. The word "composite" is not defined either.
- What the stage does well: lists of 2, then 2 3, then 2 3 5 and so on give 3, 7, 31, 211, 2311, all prime and none on the list, so the pattern "product + 1 is a new prime" builds (and matches my guess), and the 13 step breaks it: 59 and 509, neither in the list. The "Try it first" button stays locked at step 5 (screenshot shows the list 2..11 and 2311 prime) and only the last add unlocks the reveal, so the surprise is on the critical path. The reveal is not reachable without it.
- A trap: for five steps in a row the stage supports "product + 1 is prime", the very belief many people carry; the surprise at 13 is a legitimate fix, but a learner who stops early would leave with the misconception reinforced. The lock prevents that.
- Reveal earned? For 30031 = 59 × 509 yes. For "no list holds every prime: the primes never run out", the leap is made in two sentences: "so some prime divides it: one the list missed." That is the whole argument and relies on "every number above 1 has a prime factor", which the stage did not show and a beginner may not trust.
- Layout: the card sits in the middle of a large empty stage; the prime buttons are small and uncaptioned (they toggle list membership).
- Problems: (1) the options probe one number's property rather than the hook's question; (2) "remainder 1" is claimed, never shown; (3) "composite" undefined; (4) the leap from one counterexample to "primes never run out" is compressed.
- No FATAL.

## 2. After reading the brief

- The brief's boundary says the learner already knows every whole number above 1 breaks into primes (the prior lesson one-recipe-for-sixty); that is exactly the step my beginner lacked for the final leap, so for the lesson's own learner finding (4) is smaller.
- The misconception ("a long enough list could be complete") is the hook's, but the three options do not test it; only the reveal's last paragraph addresses it.
- The objective needs the remainder-1 fact; the lesson states it without showing it. That is the core idea, so it deserves a visible instance.
- Claims verified.
