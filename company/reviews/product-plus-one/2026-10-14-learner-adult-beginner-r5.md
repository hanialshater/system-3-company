# Learner review (re-review r5): product-plus-one
lesson version: v2
boundary: an adult who remembers basic school arithmetic and everyday life; knows roughly what a prime is; has not heard Euclid's argument.
Screenshots seen: yes (artifacts/walkthroughs/product-plus-one/sheet.png)
Verdict: launch

## 1. From the artifact

- Predict: "Product plus one", with the hook "Primes thin out as numbers grow. Could a long enough list hold them all?" The question gives 30031 already computed and offers three statements. "It is prime" is the classic misreading of Euclid, so it is a good trap. Small logic snag: if 30031 were prime, "it has a prime factor not in the list" would also be true (itself), so options 1 and 3 aren't exclusive in general. Only this number's being composite makes 3 the unique answer. The locked stage (empty card, prime chips) leaks nothing.
- Explore: adding primes one at a time builds a card: LIST, PRODUCT + 1 (3, 7, 31, 211, 2,311, 30,031), ITS PRIMES, and a remainder row ("2→1 3→1 5→1 ..."). Five times "prime itself, not in the list" builds the expectation that it is always prime. Then 13 breaks it: "59, 509: not prime, and none of its primes are in the list." That is a lovely set-up and payoff, entirely on stage. The remainder row showing →1 for every listed prime is the mechanism, made visible.
- Reveal: "30031 = 59 × 509, primes not in the list" was seen. "Product + 1 leaves remainder 1 for each" was seen. "Every whole number above 1 breaks into primes" is told. It is the one premise the stage doesn't show, and an adult beginner can accept it from school. "Works for any finite list... (Euclid)" is the generalisation, stated; the step is short and honest.
- Layout: the stage has a large empty area above the card in every frame, and the card floats mid-stage. It is cosmetic only.

## 2. Earlier findings (2026-10-13 review, v1, verdict revise)

1. The options probe one number's property rather than the hook's question: **partly fixed**. The question now opens "Could 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13 be every prime?", which ties the number to the list. The options are unchanged (see my new finding 1 on their overlap).
2. "Remainder 1" claimed, never shown: **fixed**. The stage now shows a remainder row for each listed prime (2→1 3→1 ... 13→1) at every step. This was the core finding.
3. "Composite" undefined: **fixed**. The stage now says "not prime".
4. The leap from one counterexample to "primes never run out" is compressed and relies on prime factorisation: **not fixed**, minor. The brief's learner is said to know factorisation already (the earlier review's section 2).
5. Large empty stage, small uncaptioned buttons: **not fixed**, cosmetic.

## 3. New findings

1. Minor: options 1 and 3 overlap in logic (a prime is its own "prime factor not in the list"). Rewording option 3 as "It is not prime, and its prime factors are new" or similar would make the choice clean.
2. Minor: the empty space above the card on phone wastes a third of the stage.
3. Minor: "every whole number above 1 breaks into primes" is the one told premise. One option is a line on stage when 59 × 509 appears ("every number splits into primes; here they are").
4. No FATAL.
