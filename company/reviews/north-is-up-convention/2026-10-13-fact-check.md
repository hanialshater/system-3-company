| c3 | verified | The Hereford map, like other medieval European world maps, has east at the top. | Hereford has east at the top (Wikipedia; Hereford Cathedral). 'Like other medieval European world maps' is accepted as 'many/typical' (T-O mappae mundi are east-oriented; this is where 'orient' comes from) - it is not claimed for all; wording note: 'many' is safer than an implied 'all'. Stage derives east-at-top from the projection. |
| c4 | verified | The Hereford map is centred on Jerusalem. | Jerusalem is the centre of the Hereford map (Wikipedia; Hereford Cathedral). Stage centre 35.2E 31.8N matches Jerusalem. |
| c5 | verified | The word 'orient' comes from the Latin for 'rising', the east, where the Sun rises. | Latin oriens, orientis 'rising, east', present participle of oriri 'to rise' (OED; Lewis and Short). |
| c6 | verified | Al-Idrisi drew his world map in 1154 with south at the top. | Tabula Rogeriana, completed 1154 for Roger II, is oriented south-up (Wikipedia 'Tabula Rogeriana'; Dickinson College and map-dealer descriptions). Surviving copies are later but the map was drawn in 1154. |
| c7 | verified | North at the top is today's convention for almost every map. | Searches (UCSB Geography 'Why is North Up on Maps', GeoGarage, Flowing Data) agree north-up is a convention that was settled in Europe by the early 1500s and is the default for modern maps. 'Almost every' is a general statement about default practice; some maps are deliberately oriented otherwise (track-up navigation, local wayfinding). Non-blocking. |
