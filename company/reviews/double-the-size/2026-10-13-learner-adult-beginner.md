# Learner review: double-the-size
lesson version: v1
boundary: an adult who remembers basic school arithmetic and everyday life; has never studied the topic (knows area of a square = side x side vaguely; has ordered pizza).

## 1. From the artifact

- Prediction: a real guess. Everyday intuition says "twice as wide, twice as much" (2) and 3 and 4 are the plausible rivals. Good tension; the hook ("Is it twice as much pizza?") invites exactly the wrong answer. Stage while locked shows only the small pizza, so nothing leaks.
- Where understanding first broke: step 1. The slider jumped to "24 inches", past the 12 inches the question is about, and the caption says "4× as wide and 4× as tall: 4 × 4 = 16 small boxes." I was told to "Grow the pizza to 12 inches" but my first stop was 24; the 12-inch case (answer 4) is the one I care about and I was not sure I had seen it. The slider moves in whole multiples, so 12 exists, but the driver's path never showed it.
- The key picture (final screenshot) is 16 small circles in a 4 x 4 grid inside a box with a dashed big circle. The small circles visibly leave white gaps and do not fill the big circle: "16 six-inch pizzas fill the box just as much as one 24-inch" is true of the BOXES but a beginner may think, looking at the white gaps, that the small pizzas hold less than the big one. The reveal's "A pizza fills the same share of its box at any size" is what repairs this, but it comes after the picture, and is asserted.
- Terms: the stage says "small boxes" (an unexplained unit; boxes, not pizzas) while the question says area. The step from "boxes" to "area" is never named until the reveal.
- Reveal gate: reachable (after growing the pizza and filling). Reveal earned: mostly; the grid count is shown and the 2 x 2 = 4 case follows the same pattern, but the generalisation "three times as wide is 9 times; this works for any shape" is not shown.
- Problems: (1) the 12-inch case, the one asked, is not the path's landmark; (2) white gaps between small circles undercut "same amount of pizza" visually; (3) "small boxes" vs "area" unexplained; (4) "any shape" claim unshown.
- No FATAL.

## 2. After reading the brief

- The brief's misconception ("doubling every length doubles area") is exactly what the hook and option 2 set up; well aimed.
- The brief assumes the learner knows area as tiles covering a shape; that is the "small boxes" idea, but the lesson counts boxes, not pizzas, so tile-counting is by proxy, which is where my gap complaint sits.
- Claims all verified; no false statement seen.

Verdict: revise
