# Learner review: one-recipe-for-sixty
lesson version: v1
boundary: an adult who remembers basic school arithmetic and everyday life; has never studied the topic (knows multiplication tables and may recall "prime" as numbers like 2, 3, 5, 7; has not thought about unique factorisation).

## 1. From the artifact

- Prediction: weak tension. The hook asks "Does where you start matter?" and the stage tells me to split 60 "any way you like"; the shape of the question points to "no, it comes out the same", so option 1 is the one an everyday reader picks by tone. 2 and 3 are filler. A real guess is possible only for a reader who has not noticed this pattern, and the visible factor buttons (2 × 30 ... 6 × 10) already hint at how few possibilities there are.
- Where understanding first broke: nowhere in the logic. The tree is clear and the colour coding (every 2 blue, every 3 green, every 5 yellow) lets me see at once that the shelf of five rows has the same four coloured dots. The one soft spot is the word "primes", used in the hook, predict and caption without a definition; I half-remember it, and the lesson does not tell me why splitting stops at 2, 3 and 5 (they cannot be split).
- The visual is the strongest part: the final screenshot shows 6 × 10 tree plus a shelf listing 2×30, 3×20, 4×15, 5×12, 6×10, each with 2·2·3·5. The "Every start ends in 2 · 2 · 3 · 5" caption appears after the third start, so by the fifth button the point is repeated.
- Reveal earned? For 60, yes. The reveal then generalises: "Every whole number above 1 breaks into primes in exactly one way." That is based on one number; I saw nothing about any other number, and a beginner might think 60 is special ("That is no accident with 60" tells me it is not). It is a plain assertion rather than something shown.
- "Your prediction held." is a bit flat for someone who guessed 1, but harmless.
- The panel says "Start 60 three different ways"; the screenshot shows five buttons and all five were used. No dead control seen.
- Problems: (1) the prediction's wording leans to the right answer; (2) "prime" undefined; (3) the any-number generalisation rests on a single case; (4) every tree is only two levels deep, so no case where a learner chooses a different split deeper down.
- No FATAL.

## 2. After reading the brief

- The brief's boundary includes knowing what a prime is, so the undefined-"prime" gap is a boundary mismatch only for my looser beginner; against the lesson's own learner it is fine.
- The misconception ("different ways depending on the first split") is exactly what the five-button shelf tests; but with the hook phrased as "does where you start matter?" the stage nearly names the answer, so the prediction may not be exposing the misconception for people who hold it.
- The objective includes the any-number generalisation, which the stage cannot demonstrate with one number; that is an inherent limit worth a human check.
- One claim is still unverified in meta.json.

Verdict: revise
