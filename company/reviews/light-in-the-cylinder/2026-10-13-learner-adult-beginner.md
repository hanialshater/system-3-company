# Learner review: light-in-the-cylinder
lesson version: v2
boundary: an adult who has used maps and a globe and knows the continents; has never studied geography or map projections.

## 1. From the artifact

- Prediction: a real guess, but the question is hard to hold: "Put a light at the Earth's centre and wrap paper in a cylinder touching the equator. Measured in globe radii up from the equator, where does 80°N land?" "Globe radii" is a new unit and the options (1.0, 1.4, 5.7) are not guessable; 1.0 is the "up to the top of the globe" instinct. The hook ("Some map projections are literally shadows cast onto paper") introduces projection by metaphor, which helps. The locked stage already draws the globe, a vertical yellow paper line with a 1-6 scale, a ray at 60°N hitting about 1.7, and "ALONG THE GLOBE 1.05 radii": a free worked example before I answer, though ON THE PAPER shows "?".
- Where understanding first broke: the scale. I had to work out that the numbers on the yellow line are radii of the globe and that the pink dot is "where the 80°N point lands". The side-on view is not obviously a cross-section of a globe; "side view, to scale" helps a little.
- What was SHOWN: sliding to 80°N sends the ray steeply up to 5.67 on the paper while the green arc on the globe is only 1.40 radii. Then "Flat sheet on the pole" lays a horizontal line on top, and the same point lands at 0.18 (versus 0.17 along the globe): a clear contrast: the stretch moves from the pole to the equator. That second picture is good.
- Reveal vs stage: 5.67 and 1.40 on the stage. NOT shown: "tan(latitude): 1.73 at 60°N" (the 1.73 was visible in the locked picture only as a position, with no number) and "the pole itself would never land" (the slider stops at 80°N). The reveal's last paragraph ("a projection is a rule for putting each point of the globe on paper") is a definition told after use; that sentence is the point of the lesson.
- The "paper rolled round the equator" and the flat sheet pictures do not show a flat map of the world, so a beginner may not connect "radii up the paper" with a map of continents.
- Phone legibility: the globe is small and the labels "light" and "equator" sit on top of the globe outline; the tick numbers are small but readable; the slider shows an orange focus box. In the flat-sheet state a large empty area sits above the picture. Reveal clipped at the bottom in the screenshot (mid-sentence).
- Reveal earned: the number yes (seen on the stage); the formula and the projection definition told.
- Problems: (1) "globe radii" unit and the unusual set-up; (2) the free 60°N example shows ALONG THE GLOBE but not the paper reading; (3) tan and the pole told; (4) never connected to an actual map.
- No FATAL.


## 2. After reading the brief

- Brief says the learner knows projections exist; the light-and-paper model is a good bridge and the 5.67 vs 1.40 readout shows the stretch. The tan formula and the link to a real map are told only; 'radii' unit is unexplained.
- Brief misconception: Projections are arbitrary redrawings, with no geometry behind them.
