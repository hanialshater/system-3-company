Shared idea: counting unordered pairs, n(n-1)/2, grows much faster than n.

## Give a first-time learner: B, by a wide margin.

- **A never shows the number its title promises.** "Six socks are not six possible pairs": on the walkthrough path the learner taps "Marked partners" (3 candidate pairs) and moves the count to 10 (5 pairs). The all-possible-pairs count (15 for 6 socks, 45 for 10) is never on screen; at predict the "Candidate pairs" readout is cut off at the stage bottom. The contrast the lesson exists for is invisible.
- **A's question has an obvious answer.** "Which method checks fewer pairs?" with "Marked partners" as an option: matching by a unique mark is fewer checks by definition. There is no misconception to break; the surprise (how fast all-pairs grows) is not what is asked.
- **A's reveal is abstract and repeats itself.** "Not quite: it was ..." then "Not quite." again; "The combinatorial growth explains the appeal of distinctive patterns" names a growth the learner never saw. At the reveal the sock picture's top row is cropped.
- **B builds the count with the learner.** Each tap brings in one person and draws their lines; the caption keeps the running sum 1 + 2 + ... + 9. Then "Count lines at each person" puts a 9 at every node: 10 x 9 = 90 line ends, two per handshake. Two routes to 45 on one picture, and the reveal names the 100 trap (shaking your own hand). The full graph and the reveal fit together on the phone frame.
- **B's problem:** nine near-identical taps before the second count; a learner who sees the pattern by person 5 has no shortcut, which strains the 60 seconds.

Recommendation (witness): A should not ship in this form; the fix is a problem, not a test: learners never see how many pairs "all possible" means. B is the concept's lesson.
