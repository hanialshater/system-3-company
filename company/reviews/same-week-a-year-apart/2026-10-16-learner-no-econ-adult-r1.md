## Findings (written from the walkthrough before reading build.md or meta.json)

1. **Where understanding first broke: the hook and the question ask two different things.** The hook asks about me ("Would you wait a week for $10 more?"). The question asks about a hypothetical steady discounter ("someone prefers $110 at week 53 ... they must"). The explore then asks for my own choices again. This mind arrives thinking the lesson is about what *I* would do, answers a logic question about a model, then plays a game about itself. The lesson never says which of the two the verdict line judges. It judges the model question only (the kit compares the option index), but the reveal opens with my choices ("Both a year out and today you waited ..."), right under "Your prediction held." On the walker's path (predict wait, choose wait twice) these line up by luck. On other paths they collide:
   - **Take-first learner** (takes $100 at week 52). The strip says "Steady model: take $100". After the reveal the verdict says "Not quite: it was 'Wait a week again for the $110'" if they predicted anything else, or "Your prediction held" if they predicted wait. Either way the stage they just played never showed the question's premise (someone who waits at week 52). The option text "Wait a week again" drops the condition "because they waited at week 52", so a learner who took the $100 sees the answer "wait" next to their own stage, where the steady model said "take $100". Nothing they did tested what they predicted.
   - **Option 2 learner who then reverses** (waits at week 52, takes $100 today). Their prediction ("take the $100, since today weighs more") described their own behaviour, and the reveal agrees that their behaviour is present bias, yet the verdict line says "Not quite". No sentence says "you predicted what people often do; the question asked what the steady model does". This mind reads it as "I was right and was told I was wrong".

2. **What the prediction felt like: mostly a wording check, with one real distractor.** The caption above the question says "A simple model: each week counts the same", and option 2 says "since today weighs more", which contradicts "steady" on its face. So option 2 is ruled out by the words, and option 1 is close to stated by the caption. Option 3 ("Either, depending on their discount rate") is the honest misconception: this mind thinks "a very impatient person would grab the $100 today". That is a good distractor. But "steady discounting" and "discount rate" are undefined when the prediction is asked. "Each week counts the same" reads to a lay adult as "time doesn't matter", not "each week of delay costs the same share". With that reading option 1 is right for the wrong reason (nobody would ever take $100), and then the strip "Steady model: take $100" in step 1 contradicts it with no explanation. For the "quiz about wording or one decision" question: the stage reads as one decision made twice (good), the prediction reads as a wording quiz for options 1 and 2.

3. **Whether the reveal answered the question this learner had: not for the wrong answers.** The reveal text is the same for every prediction (Demo.tsx's reveal ignores the prediction; only the kit's one verdict line changes). The question an option-3 learner actually has is "why doesn't the rate matter?" The reveal says "judges the pair only by its gap, one week for $10 more, and the gap never changed", which is the right idea, but it never says the missing step in this mind's words: *whatever the rate, the same rate decides both pairs, so a person impatient enough to take $100 today would already have taken it at week 52*. The question's premise rules that person out, and the reveal does not point at the premise. An option-3 learner leaves with "it was wait" and no reason they can repeat.

4. **Present bias is told, not seen, for consistent choosers.** If I wait twice (or take twice), the stage never shows a reversal, and present bias arrives as one defining sentence ("Waiting, then taking the $100 today, would fit only a model that gives today extra weight"). Many first-time learners will choose consistently while knowing they are being tested. Not blocking by itself: the walk is honest about it ("Where it stops: ... with your first choice in view"), which I liked.

5. **The strip "Steady model: ..." does not say what it forecasts.** After my week-52 choice it reads "Steady model: take $100" or "Steady model: wait". It never says "at every date" or "today too", and it does not react when my today choice differs (step 4: "today ✓ $100" sits under "Steady model: wait" with no mismatch cue until the reveal). A learner can read it as the model echoing the pair in view. Note, not blocking.

6. **Slider direction is inverted against the timeline.** The timeline runs today (left) to week 52 (right). Dragging "Pair starts" to the right moves the coins to the left. Two stops (week 52 → week 13 → today), readout at the right. This mind hesitated at step 3; it got there because the readout says "today". Note, not blocking.

7. **At "today" the two leader lines land on one point.** $100 today and $110 in a week converge on the same dot at the left end (steps 3-5, reveal); at week 52 both land at the right end. The "1 week, +$10" bracket carries the gap, so it is readable, but the picture says "same day" at both stops. Note.

8. **What worked.** One stage, one pair, one slider; both choices stay marked on the axis (✓ $110 week 52, today ✓ $110), so the comparison is in view at the reveal. "Where it stops" names its own limit. Every label was readable on sheet.png (font sizes not measured). No dead end: "Show me why" unlocked after the today choice.

Verdict reason: revise. The reveal speaks to the walker's path only: for the two wrong predictions (option 3 "depending on their discount rate", option 2 with a reversing learner) and for any take-first learner, the verdict line and the stage disagree, and no sentence explains why the rate cannot change the answer or that the question was about the model, not about you (findings 1 and 3).
