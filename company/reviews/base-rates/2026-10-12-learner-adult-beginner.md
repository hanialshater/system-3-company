*Simulated learner. This review finds problems; it is not evidence that anyone learned.*

Boundary used: an adult who remembers school arithmetic and everyday life; has never studied probability, Bayes or medical-test statistics.

## 1. From the artifact (written before reading the brief)

**Where understanding first broke.** In the reveal: "In 1,000 people, about 1 is sick and tests positive. But 999 are healthy, and the test wrongly flags 1% of them: about 10 false alarms." Before that, in exploring, the learner's last screen reads "108 tested positive. 99 of them are actually sick." That is the opposite of the lesson's point: it looks like the 99% guess was right. The walkthrough learner tapped "1 in 100" then "1 in 10", never the scenario in the question ("1 in 1,000"), and ended on a screen that confirms "99 of 108". Then the reveal says "Not quite: it was 9%" about a case the learner never saw. Understanding breaks right there: "I counted and it was 99 out of 108, why am I told 9%?"

**Did the prediction allow a real guess?** Yes in form (four options, hook "99% accurate so 99% likely?" is the exact intuition). But the question packs three numbers (1 in 1,000, "catches 99%", "clears 99%") into one sentence; "clears 99% of healthy ones" is jargon that a layperson may not map to "1% of healthy people test positive". The locked stage is an empty grid with "Make a prediction to start", fine.

**Did exploring answer the learner's actual question?** The real question is "if the test is 99% right, why isn't a positive 99% likely?". The grid of 1,000 squares with red/yellow dots is a good concrete answer when it is at 1 in 1,000. But:
- The panel says "Fill the town to 1,000, test everyone, and count the positives" and never says to set the disease to 1 in 1,000 (the question's setting). The default highlighted chip is not explained, and the two chips the learner is drawn to are the two easy ones.
- At 1 in 100 or 1 in 10 the picture mostly shows red (sick, positive) dots; the yellow false alarms are a minority, so the lesson's key observation (false alarms swamp true positives) is visible only at 1 in 1,000, where a single red dot among about 10 yellow in a 1,000-cell grid is easy to miss, and the legend colour "sick, missed" (pale pink) is almost invisible.
- Nothing prompts the comparison across the three chips. The learner can see only the reassuring case.
- The "Test all" button turned into "Hide results" with no explanation, and the counts change from "99 of 108" text that I read as "99% right".

**Reveal earned or told?** Told, for the learner who explored the wrong cases. The 9% comes by arithmetic in prose (1 vs 10), and the 1-in-100 and 1-in-10 figures (50%, 92%) are stated even though the learner's own screen said 99 of 108 (that is 92%, which matches, but they are never told "that is what you saw"). The Harvard anecdote (95% vs "about 2%") is a different question (5% false positives), told as an aside; a beginner will be confused by "2%" next to "9%".

**Findings (problems):**
1. The exploration can end on a screen where "99 of 108 are actually sick" supports the learner's wrong answer, and the reveal then contradicts it for a setting they did not look at.
2. The panel instruction does not send the learner to the question's own setting (1 in 1,000), so the key picture can be skipped entirely.
3. At the key setting, the one true positive is a single dot lost among 1,000 cells; the contrast with the ~10 false alarms depends on noticing it.
4. "Catches 99% of sick people and clears 99% of healthy ones" is hard to parse; the hook says "99% accurate", the question uses two different statements.
5. The Harvard line uses a different false-positive rate and a different answer (2%), told not shown; it may read as a contradiction with 9%.
6. "Sick, missed" legend colour is nearly invisible and the missed case is never explained.
7. After "Test all" turns into "Hide results", it is unclear that the learner can change the disease chip and re-test.

**FATAL: reveal can be reached without seeing the key thing.** A learner can complete exploring on 1 in 10 (as the walkthrough did), see "108 tested positive. 99 of them are actually sick", which reads as confirmation of 99%, and reach a reveal that claims 9% without ever having looked at 1 in 1,000. This is also, for that path, a reinforcement of the misconception.

## 2. After reading the brief

- Brief intent: the learner counts a population of 1,000 and sees that, for a rare disease, most positives are healthy people. The experience delivers this only at the 1 in 1,000 setting, and nothing steers the learner there; the other chips, which the brief treats as extension, are the most inviting and show the opposite pattern.
- The brief's boundary (knows percentages and "1 in 1,000", not sensitivity or Bayes) matches my reading: the lesson avoids jargon in the reveal, but the question's "catches 99% ... clears 99%" phrasing is the one place it leans on it.
- The recall (1 in 10,000, about 1 in 100) is the same structure; a learner who never saw 1 in 1,000 has no picture to carry into it.
- The misconception is named correctly ("99% accurate means 99% likely"); for the wandering learner the exploring step feeds it.

Verdict: fatal
