Related ideas, not the same: B is a dominant strategy (prisoner's dilemma); A is a mixed-strategy equilibrium (improve a side, use it less). Both are 2x2 payoff tables with toggles.

## Give a first-time learner: B first, A second. A has the bigger surprise; B is the one a newcomer can read.

- **A's result is the stronger "predict and be wrong" moment.** "Better at shooting left: shoot left more?" and the answer is less (44.4%). The "Keep 50% left" control is the key move: it shows the keeper switching to dive left 100% and capping the kicker at 0.700. That is the mechanism made visible, not told.
- **A asks a lot before the learner has touched anything.** The question is about 44 words and contains the setup, a premise ("50% left is best") and the change. At predict the stage is shrunk so the table and bars use text a phone reader cannot read. Scores are probabilities written as 0.50 / 0.90 / 0.722; a first-timer has to translate "scores 0.722" into "scores 72% of the time".
- **A's reveal frame crops the top of the stage** (the "Scoring" line and column headers are off screen) and the reveal is about 90 words, with three decimal numbers per sentence. The final sentence ("either dive concedes 0.722 and either kick wins 0.722") is the equilibrium idea; it is correct and dense.
- **B is legible at every frame.** Big cells, "+2" / "+1" badges on the better row for each column, a "You get" readout; the reveal walks the two columns, then the twist (both reason the same, both get 1). Its problem: the predict question restates the whole payoff table in prose (32 words) that the stage already shows, and the reveal crops the column headers.
- **Order matters here:** A's reveal leans on "neither side gains by switching", which B's lesson sets up. As a pair they are a sequence, not substitutes.

Recommendation (witness): keep both; place B before A on any NCM path. A's problems for revision: learners cannot read the predict-frame table, and decimals-as-scores slow the first read.
